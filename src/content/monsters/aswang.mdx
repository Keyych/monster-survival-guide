<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is the middle of the night in a barangay somewhere in the Western Visayas. The house is
    timber and tin, the shutters are closed, and above the door hangs a head of garlic that has been
    there so long it has gone to paper. A knife rests under the pillow — point out, the way the
    grandmother insisted — and a stingray tail hangs from a nail in the corner. Outside, the frogs
    stop.
  </p>
  <p>
    Somewhere off in the dark there is a sound people here have a name for: a soft, wet flapping, or
    a faint <em>tik-tik</em> that grows quieter as the thing making it draws nearer. Everyone in the
    house knows the rule. You do not open the shutter to look. You wait for morning, and in the
    morning you ask who in the village has been keeping to themselves lately.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirteenth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject: the Aswang —
    not a single monster but a whole family of them, the most feared beings in Filipino folklore.
    This is also an entry that has to be careful, because unlike the cold of the north or a sick
    coyote in a Texas field, the real danger in the aswang's story has usually fallen on living
    human beings.
  </p>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The aswang is sometimes called the national monster of the Philippines, and it is genuinely
    ancient — its roots reach back into Visayan belief from long before Ferdinand Magellan reached
    the islands in 1521. It is not one creature with one description. It is an umbrella word,{' '}
    <em>asuang</em>
    in the older spelling, stretched across a crowd of distinct beings that different islands and
    different barangays describe in different ways. Ask ten people what an aswang is and you may get
    ten answers, all of them correct to the person giving them.
  </p>
  <p>
    The folklorist Maximo Ramos, whose 1990 study remains the standard reference, sorted that crowd
    into roughly five types: the blood-sucker, the corpse-eater or ghoul, the viscera-sucker (the
    famous self-segmenting manananggal among them), the animal-transformer or weredog, and the
    sorcerer or witch who sickens neighbours by supernatural means. Scholars are careful to note
    that this is a twentieth-century comparative scheme, a way of organising the stories — not a
    taxonomy every village ever recognised.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Most of the monsters in this guide hide in the wilderness. The aswang hides in the village — in
    the quiet neighbour, the widow who fishes alone, the old man who keeps strange hours. That is
    what makes it the most human horror in these pages, and the most dangerous to be accused of
    being.
  </div>
  <p>
    No province is more bound to the aswang than Capiz, on the island of Panay, which carries a
    nationwide reputation as "aswang country." Historians of the belief suspect that reputation owes
    as much to old ethnolinguistic rivalry — Capiz against neighbouring Iloilo — as to anything
    supernatural, and some say the real folkloric hub lies elsewhere on Panay entirely. A further
    strand of scholarship links the whole cluster of beliefs to the Spanish colonial habit of
    demonising Filipinos — often women, and often healers — who resisted conversion. The monster, in
    other words, has a history as well as a mythology.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The aswang is not frozen in the past. Capiz has at times leaned into its reputation — a
      planned "Aswang Festival" in Roxas City in the mid-2000s drew real political, economic, and
      religious pushback, documented by folklorists, over whether a province should celebrate,
      profit from, or bury a label its own residents can still find stigmatising. Living folklore is
      argued over, not merely inherited.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    There is no single thing to look for, because the aswang wears whatever shape its particular
    form demands. By day, tradition holds, it is indistinguishable from an ordinary person — a
    neighbour, a vendor, a relative. The tells are small and folkloric: eyes that reflect your own
    image upside down, a preference for walking with the body oddly angled, a reclusiveness that the
    community reads backward into every later misfortune.
  </p>
  <p>
    By night the forms diverge. The manananggal severs its torso from its lower half and flies on
    membranous wings, trailing its entrails, leaving the abandoned legs standing somewhere to be
    found and salted. The weredog and its kin take the shape of a dog, a pig, a cat, or a great
    black bird. The viscera-suckers are associated with that thin <em>tik-tik</em> or{' '}
    <em>wakwak</em> call — a sound said, confusingly, to grow fainter as the creature comes closer,
    so that distance itself becomes untrustworthy.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      The most important thing to understand about "spotting" an aswang is what the spotting does.
      In the recorded cases, the accusation lands on real, identifiable people — and the people
      chosen are almost always the socially weak: a disabled elder, a widow, an outsider. When you
      think you are hunting a monster in a Visayan village, the record says you are far more likely
      to be hurting a neighbour.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    Set aside the question of whether anything flies at night, and the aswang does something very
    precise inside a community: it explains. When a pregnancy ends without warning, when a child
    sickens and dies, when a healthy adult wastes away for reasons no one can see, the aswang gives
    the loss a shape and a culprit. Folklorists have long read the tradition as a way of making
    sense of the cruelties of a hard world — illness, miscarriage, sudden death — in an idiom older
    than the clinic.
  </p>
  <p>
    It also polices. Aswang belief encodes warnings: do not wander alone at night, guard pregnant
    women and newborns, keep the house closed after dark, and — crucially — be a known and trusted
    part of your community, because the person who keeps apart is the person who gets named. The
    monster rewards belonging and punishes isolation, which is a social instruction dressed as a
    horror story.
  </p>
  <p>
    And that same machinery can be turned against people deliberately. An accusation of being aswang
    runs in families in the telling, which means it can isolate not just a person but a bloodline.
    The documented aswang cases are, over and over, cases of ordinary human beings being marked,
    avoided, and sometimes driven out — the belief operating exactly as a mechanism of exclusion.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    Here is the honest part, and it is the whole point of this entry: there is nothing to fight. No
    specimen of any aswang has ever been recovered, frozen, photographed with scale, or tested. This
    is folklore — rich, ancient, regionally precise folklore — not a cryptid awaiting a carcass.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The protections are cultural, not proven.</strong> Garlic over the door, salt, a blade
    beneath the pillow, the stingray-tail whip — these recur across the Visayan record and matter
    enormously as custom and comfort. There is no evidence they repel anything but anxiety, and that
    is not nothing.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The footprint, where it is real, is social and historical.</strong> The aswang's genuine
    power is what belief does: it discriminates, it excludes, and — as the record below shows — it
    can be weaponised by people who understand it. That footprint is far more consequential than any
    fanged silhouette.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of that makes the tradition false in the ways that matter to the people who hold it. It
    means the thing to respect is the culture, and the thing to be wary of is what the fear does to
    the living.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two cases show the aswang doing its real work. The first is the tradition itself —
    centuries deep, documented by folklorists, and alive enough to still start arguments. The second
    is the day a modern military turned that belief into a weapon.
  </p>

    <p>
      The aswang is one of the oldest and most widespread beings in Filipino folklore, rooted in
      Visayan belief that predates Spanish contact in 1521. Across the archipelago it functions as
      an umbrella term for several distinct creatures — the viscera-sucking manananggal, the ghoul,
      the weredog, the witch — which Maximo Ramos organised in 1990 into the five-type scheme still
      used today. Capiz province on Panay carries the enduring, nationwide reputation as its
      heartland.
    </p>
    <p>
      Scholars who have gone looking at the belief up close keep finding the same thing. In a study
      published in the <em>Kyoto Review of Southeast Asia</em>, Kentaro Azuma traced aswang rumours
      near Roxas City to two living villagers — a widow who fished for a living and an elderly
      disabled man — and showed how the label sanctioned their social exclusion. The aswang, on the
      record, is less a creature in the dark than a story a community tells about the people it has
      decided to fear.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      During the campaign against the communist Hukbalahap rebellion in the early 1950s, US Air
      Force officer and CIA operative Edward Lansdale set out to deny the Huks a hill they were
      using. Rather than fight for it, he exploited what the local population already believed. His
      psywar squad first spread word through the area that an aswang had taken up residence on the
      hill.
    </p>
    <p>
      Then they made the rumour true. The squad ambushed a Huk patrol after dark, seized the last
      man in the column, punctured his neck with two holes in the manner of the folklore, drained
      the body of blood, and left it on the trail for his comrades to find. By Lansdale's own
      account in his 1972 memoir, <em>In the Midst of Wars</em>, "every member of the patrol
      believed that the asuang had got him." The unit abandoned the hill.
    </p>
    <p>
      The episode is a genuine, documented piece of Cold War history, and it is the clearest proof
      of the aswang's real power: not fangs, but fear — a belief so deeply held that a foreign
      military could pick it up and use it as a weapon against the people who held it. Lansdale
      would carry the same playbook of belief-as-leverage to Vietnam.
    </p>
