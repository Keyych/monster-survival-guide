<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is late, and the house is quiet, and someone in it is old or ill or simply asleep. Then it
    comes from outside the walls — a cry that is not a dog, not the wind, not a neighbour. A woman's
    voice, raised in a lament that rises and falls and will not resolve, keening for a grief that
    has not yet arrived. In the morning you will understand what it was for.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is not a creature you fight, and there is no advice in these pages about bolts or blades or
    fire. The Irish bean sídhe is an omen, not a predator. She does not come to take anyone. She
    comes to tell you that someone is already being taken, and that the household has a little time
    left to gather around them.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the sixteenth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the Banshee —
    the keening woman, the fairy-woman who weeps for the dying of certain Gaelic families. Treat
    this one gently. It sits closer to real grief than almost anything else in this guide.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The word is Irish — <em>bean sídhe</em>, from Old Irish <em>ben síde</em>, "woman of the fairy
    mound." It ties her to the <em>síde</em>, the ancient tumuli that dot the Irish countryside and
    that pre-Christian tradition held to be the dwellings of the Otherworld. She is not a demon and
    not a ghost in the ordinary sense. She is a being of the old order, attached across generations
    to the living families of Gaelic Ireland.
  </p>
  <p>
    Her cry is bound to a real human custom. Keening — <em>caoineadh</em>, "weeping" — was the
    traditional Irish lament for the dead, performed aloud at a wake by a mourning woman, the{' '}
    <em>bean chaointe</em>, sometimes hired for the purpose. Folk belief held that a fairy woman
    sang that same lament, and that she would sing it when a family member died or was about to,
    even if the death happened far from home. In that case her wail was the household's first news
    of it.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Most of this guide is about things that want to harm you. The banshee wants nothing from you at
    all. She is grief given a voice and sent on ahead — a warning delivered out of what the
    tradition frames almost as loyalty to a bloodline.
  </div>
  <p>
    She does not attach to everyone. By long tradition she laments only the old Gaelic families —
    those whose surnames carry the <em>Ó</em> or <em>Mac</em> prefix that marks native descent, with
    a few recorded exceptions for families "gifted in music and song." Britannica notes the belief
    that she warns only families of old Irish stock; certain lineages have their visits from her
    chronicled down the centuries as a matter of record rather than rumour.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The keening tradition was real and widespread enough that the Catholic Church repeatedly moved
      to suppress professional keening women at wakes between the 17th and 19th centuries. The
      banshee belief grew in the same soil — not as a children's fright, but as the supernatural
      echo of a formal, communal way of mourning that has largely now been lost.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    Accounts of her appearance do not fully agree, which is itself worth knowing. She is most often
    described as a woman with long, streaming hair, frequently seen combing it — some legends insist
    she can only keen while combing. She is variously young and beautiful or bent and ancient; Lady
    Wilde recorded her as small and old. Her dress is commonly a grey cloak over green, her eyes red
    from endless weeping.
  </p>
  <p>
    But the most-cited firsthand sighting, Lady Fanshawe's, describes something different: a pale
    figure with reddish hair hanging loose, appearing at a window above water. The lesson is that
    she is known far more reliably by her voice than by her face. The keen is the constant. The
    woman behind it shifts from telling to telling.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      If you hear a wail with no mourner to make it, do not assume it is meant for you. The bean
      sídhe keens for her own bloodline, and in much of the tradition she is heard before she is
      ever seen — if she is seen at all. The thing to do with the sound is not to chase it down. It
      is to count who is under your roof and who is not yet home.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The pattern in the records is consistent. First comes the cry, usually at night, usually outside
    the house — a sustained keening lament rather than a scream, often described as unbearably
    sorrowful rather than frightening. In parts of Leinster the wail is said to be strong enough to
    shatter glass. It is heard by the household, sometimes by a whole townland, before any word of a
    death has reached them.
  </p>
  <p>
    Then, within hours or a day or two, the death comes — of a member of the family she is bound to,
    wherever in the world that person happens to be. The sequence is always that way round. The keen
    precedes the loss; it does not follow it. That ordering is the whole of the belief, and the
    whole of its terror, because it means the sound is a prediction coming true.
  </p>
  <p>
    There is one escalation worth noting. Tradition holds that when several banshees are heard
    keening together at once, the death they foretell is that of someone great or holy — a figure of
    unusual importance. A single voice is for a single household. A chorus is for a nation.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    This is not a section about how to defeat her, because there is nothing here to defeat. The
    banshee takes no one and touches no one. To "survive" a banshee is simply to understand what you
    are hearing and to respond to it well.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>There is nothing to resist.</strong> She is a messenger. Fighting the message does not
    change the news, and in every account the figure cannot be caught, cornered, or driven off — she
    is seen, and then she is gone.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The sound can be mistaken.</strong> A fox's scream, a barn owl, the wind in a chimney,
    or a human keener at a nearby wake can all be taken for her. The honest field skill here is
    telling an ordinary night-sound from the thing the tradition describes.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The real work is human.</strong> If you take the warning seriously, the response is not
    defensive — it is to gather the family, send for those who are far away, and make sure no one
    who is dying does so alone. That is what the belief, at its core, was always for.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two accounts are the most-cited in the written record — one set down by a 17th-century
    Englishwoman in her own memoirs, the other preserved by the folklorist who collected the south
    of Ireland's legends. Neither is proof of the supernatural. Both are genuine documents of what
    people saw, heard, and believed.
  </p>

    <p>
      In 1642, the Englishwoman Ann, Lady Fanshawe, was staying as a guest with members of the
      O'Brien family in County Clare. She recorded the night in the memoirs she later wrote for her
      son — a sober, domestic document, not a book of ghost stories. She was woken, she wrote, by a
      horrible scream, and going to the window of her room, which overhung water, she saw a figure
      outside it.
    </p>
    <p>
      It was a pale woman, she wrote, rather handsome, with reddish hair hanging dishevelled over
      her shoulders. The figure remained for some time and then vanished with further ghastly
      shrieks. Fanshawe, badly shaken, raised it with her host the next morning.
    </p>
    <p>
      He was not surprised. A near relation of the family, he told her, had died in the castle
      during the night — an illness that, she realised, had been kept from her as a guest. The
      account is one of the earliest detailed English-language descriptions of a banshee sighting,
      and it is cited to this day precisely because it comes not from folklore collectors but from
      the witness's own hand.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      The Rev. Charles Bunworth was rector of Buttevant, County Cork, a respected clergyman and a
      famous patron of travelling harpers. He died on 14 September 1772. More than fifty years later
      his great-grandson, the Cork antiquarian Thomas Crofton Croker, set down the family's account
      of his death in <em>Fairy Legends and Traditions of the South of Ireland</em> (first published
      1825).
    </p>
    <p>
      By that account, a banshee was heard keening around the rectory roughly a week before Bunworth
      fell ill, and again on the night he died — a wailing lament circling the house, heard by those
      keeping vigil over him. The telling became one of the most widely reproduced banshee stories
      in print, carried by the popularity of Croker's collection.
    </p>
    <p>
      Croker was honest about his evidence in a way worth respecting. He named no single source,
      stating only that "there are still living credible witnesses" to what had been heard. Later
      commentators have pointed out that he was himself descended from Bunworth, making it very much
      a family legend — a reminder that even the best-recorded banshee account is a record of
      belief, faithfully kept, rather than a record of fact.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The banshee is the gentlest entry in this guide and, in some ways, the saddest. She asks
      nothing of you, takes nothing from you, and cannot be fought, because she was never the enemy.
      She is the sound a culture gave to the moment before a loss — grief arriving a few hours
      early, in a voice loyal to a bloodline.
    </p>
    <p>
      Lady Fanshawe went to the window and saw a weeping woman, and in the morning learned a
      relation had died. The household around Charles Bunworth heard a keening in the dark and,
      within days, buried him. In both cases the right response was the same, and it was never a
      weapon.
    </p>
    <p>
      So if you are ever of such a family, and you hear it — do not go out, and do not be afraid of
      her. Wake the house. Send for the ones who are far away. And sit with whoever needs sitting
      with, because the time she is giving you is short, and it is the only gift she has to give.
    </p>
    <p>End of journal entry. Mind your people. Keep the vigil. Some warnings are a kindness.</p>
  </Outro>
