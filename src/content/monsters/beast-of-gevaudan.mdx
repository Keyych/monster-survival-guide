<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is the summer of 1764, and you are a child in the Margeride, the high granite country of
    southern France. You spend your days on the open pasture with the family's cattle or sheep, a
    long way from the nearest farmhouse, because that is what children do here. The grass is thin,
    and the woods come right down to the meadows.
  </p>
  <p>
    Something has started taking people from the pastures. It is bigger than the wolves everyone
    knows, people say. It comes out of the broom and the pine at the start or the end of the day,
    and it goes for the throat. Your parents still send you out, because the animals have to eat.
    You keep a stick close and watch the treeline.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirty-fourth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the Beast
    of Gévaudan, the most heavily documented man-eater in European history. Unlike most entries in
    this guide, this one has a body count written into parish registers. It is not a ghost story. It
    is a record of real deaths and a long argument about what caused them.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    Gévaudan was an old province of southern France, roughly the modern département of Lozère, with
    the attacks spilling over into parts of Haute-Loire. In the 1760s it was poor, remote and
    mountainous, a landscape of small farms where livestock grazed on open upland and children did
    much of the herding.
  </p>
  <p>
    The first recorded death came on 30 June 1764, when a fourteen-year-old shepherd girl, Jeanne
    Boulet, was killed near Langogne. Her burial entry, written the next day, says she was buried
    without the sacraments, and it names her killer as the ferocious beast. More deaths followed
    through that summer and autumn. By early 1765 they were spread over roughly 700 square miles,
    which already suggested to some that more than one animal was involved.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The Beast did not come from folklore. It came from the burial registers and the newspapers. Its
    name came from people who could not believe an ordinary wolf would behave like this.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The story spread because of when it happened. The Seven Years' War had ended in 1763, and
      François Morénas, editor of the <em>Courrier d'Avignon</em>, began running sensational reports
      of the attacks as everyday incident stories. They made the Beast a national obsession.
      Historian Jay M. Smith, in <em>Monsters of the Gévaudan</em> (2011), argues that the Beast was
      most likely several large wolves, and that press coverage and national hysteria created the
      legend.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="What You Are Looking At">
  <StatRow

  <p>
    Nobody agrees on the exact number of victims, and the honest answer is a range. National
    Geographic gives as many as 100 dead, noting that some sources claim up to 300 victims. One
    often-cited 1987 tally counts 210 attacks, 113 deaths and 49 injuries. Other counts put the dead
    at 60 to 100. Brian Dunning of Skeptoid concludes that modern analyses support a figure of about
    100. Wherever the figure lands, most of the dead were children and young women.
  </p>
  <p>
    Witnesses described an animal about the size of a calf, sometimes said to be as big as a horse,
    with a reddish-grey coat, a dark stripe down its back and a long tail. They kept saying it was
    wolf-like but not quite a wolf; Smith sums up the reports as "like a wolf, yet not a wolf." The
    captain who led the first hunt went further and wrote that it was "a monster whose father is a
    lion."
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      Most witnesses saw it for a few seconds, while it was attacking someone, and they were
      terrified. Fear makes an animal look bigger, and in this case no one doubted the danger was
      real. Treat the size estimates and the stripes with caution. The burial records are a far more
      reliable source.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The pattern in the record is consistent and grim. The attacks fell mostly on people working
    alone or in small groups on open pasture: shepherd girls, young cowherds, women carrying out
    farm work. Accounts describe an ambush hunter that came out of cover at dawn and dusk and seized
    its victims by the throat. Cattle herds sometimes protected the people tending them.
  </p>
  <p>
    The hunting campaigns are part of the record too. Captain Jean-Baptiste Duhamel organised the
    first large hunts along military lines, with thousands of volunteers, poisoned bait and soldiers
    dressed as peasant women. In February 1765 the d'Ennevals arrived, a father and son from
    Normandy. They were professional wolf hunters, and the father claimed to have killed some 1,200
    wolves. They brought bloodhounds and achieved nothing. In June 1765 the king replaced them with
    François Antoine, his own gun-bearer and Lieutenant of the Hunt, then about seventy.
  </p>
  <p>
    Antoine got his wolf in September, and for about two months the killing stopped. Then it began
    again. Smaller numbers died through 1766 and the first half of 1767, until the hunt of June 1767
    and a second large wolf. After that, the attacks ended.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    The record does tell you what worked against this animal, which is more than most entries in
    this guide can say.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>It could be driven off.</strong> In the best-documented survivals, people stood together
    and fought back with sticks, pikes, or a blade on a pole. Cattle herds that closed ranks
    protected their herders. Several people the animal attacked lived to describe it.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>It could be killed.</strong> Dunning argues that two large wolves were killed, one in
    1765 and one in 1767, and that this is what ended the attacks. The notary Étienne Marin, who
    wrote up the 1767 animal, described it as appearing to be "a wolf, but an extraordinary one."
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The wolf explanation fits better than the alternatives.</strong> Most historians,
    including Smith and Jean-Marc Moriceau, accept wolves, perhaps several of them, as the main
    cause. Moriceau's research documents thousands of wolf attacks on people across early-modern
    France, and he has argued that comparable episodes elsewhere were sometimes worse. A wolf-dog
    hybrid is a credible refinement: Dunning thinks hybridisation likely, but there is no direct
    evidence. Biologist Karl-Hans Taake has argued for an escaped young lion. A striped hyena, a war
    dog in armour and a werewolf have all been proposed. None of these has any physical evidence
    behind it, and none is needed to explain the record.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Rabies does not fit well.</strong> A rabid wolf is aggressive, but people bitten by one
    almost always die of rabies. Many survivors of these attacks did not, which makes rabies a poor
    match for the pattern.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    The record holds about a hundred deaths, and those belong to the families who lost people, not
    to this guide. The two cases here are about the people who fought back and the hunters who
    finally stopped the attacks.
  </p>

    <p>
      On 12 January 1765, near the hamlet of Villaret in the parish of Chanaleilles, the animal
      attacked a group of eight children, aged between about eight and twelve, who were out with
      cattle. Among them was ten-year-old Jacques Portefaix. The children did not run. They stayed
      together and used sticks and pikes to drive the animal off. When word reached Versailles,
      Louis XV rewarded them: 300 livres went to Portefaix and 350 were shared among the others. By
      most accounts the crown also paid for Portefaix's education.
    </p>
    <p>
      The second account comes from that August. Accounts disagree on the exact date, but most give
      11 August 1765. Marie-Jeanne Valet, a woman of about twenty who worked as a servant to the
      parish priest of Paulhac, was crossing a stream with her sister when the animal attacked. She
      drove a blade fixed to a pole into its chest, and it fled. Antoine, the king's hunter, later
      called her the Maid of Gévaudan, and a statue of her now stands in the village of Auvers. Both
      survivals were celebrated, and both show the animal could be beaten off.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      In late September 1765, sources differ between the 20th and the 21st, François Antoine shot a
      very large grey wolf near the Abbaye des Chazes. His nephew helped finish it. The animal was
      reportedly about 1.7 metres long and weighed around 60 kilograms. Villagers said they
      recognised scars from earlier hunters' shots. It was stuffed, sent to Versailles and put on
      display, and Antoine was paid and honoured. He also killed a female and a young wolf he took
      to be part of the same family. About two months later, the attacks resumed.
    </p>
    <p>
      The end came on 19 June 1767, during a hunt organised by the Marquis d'Apcher on the slopes of
      Mont Mouchet, at a spot now called La Sogne d'Auvers. A local farmer and hunter, Jean Chastel,
      shot a large wolf. Antoine had once had him jailed during the earlier hunts. The carcass was
      cut open before a surgeon arrived. Marin's report records the head of a child's thigh bone in
      its stomach, and scars matching wounds reported from earlier encounters. By one later account
      the remains were taken to Paris, judged to be only a big wolf, and buried quickly because of
      the smell. The later story that Chastel used a blessed silver bullet is legend, not record.
      After that June, the attacks stopped.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Beast of Gévaudan is the rare entry where the danger was never in doubt. Children on open
      pasture really were killed, the priests who buried them wrote it down, and the king's hunters
      really did fail for more than a year. What remains uncertain is only the animal. It was most
      likely a wolf, or several wolves, possibly with dog in the bloodline, and it became a monster
      in the telling.
    </p>
    <p>
      The people worth remembering are the ones who survived it: eight children who would not run,
      and a young servant with a blade on a pole. They were frightened, they stayed together, they
      used whatever they could reach, and they lived.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Stay together. Keep something long within reach. And remember that the
      worst monsters in the record are usually real animals that people failed to stop.
    </p>
  </Outro>
</Section>
