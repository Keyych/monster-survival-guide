<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is late afternoon on a logging road in the Six Rivers country of Northern California, and the
    light under the firs has already gone the colour of weak tea. You are further from a paved road
    than you have ever been. Somewhere off the track there is a line of prints in the creek mud —
    deep, flat-footed, far too long — and the part of your brain that keeps you alive has gone very
    quiet and very alert all at once.
  </p>
  <p>
    Nothing comes out of the trees. That is almost always how it goes. A print, a smell, a shape at
    the edge of the headlamp, a story you will tell for the rest of your life. Then the forest closes
    over it and keeps whatever it was. This is the creature that has been seen more often, by more
    people, in more of North America than any other in this guide — and that has left behind less
    that a laboratory can hold.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the tenth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject: Bigfoot — the
    Sasquatch — the forest giant of the Pacific Northwest. It is also the entry where the honest
    danger and the honest doubt point in slightly different directions, and you need to hold both.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The name that matters came first from the people who were here first. Among the Coast Salish
    nations of what is now British Columbia, the Halq'eméylem word <em>sasq'ets</em> — roughly "wild
    man" or "hairy man" — named a being woven into the oral tradition of the Sts'ailes (Chehalis)
    people long before any newspaper took an interest. In the late 1920s an Indian agent named
    J.W. Burns, working with the Sts'ailes community, gathered their accounts and Anglicised the word
    into <em>Sasquatch</em>, publishing it for a settler audience. The creature did not begin as a
    tabloid story. It began as someone else's neighbour on the land.
  </p>
  <p>
    The other name — Bigfoot — is much younger and much more suspect. In 1958, workers on a road crew
    at Bluff Creek, California, found enormous footprints around their equipment; a local newspaper,
    the Humboldt Times, ran the story and the catchy label stuck. The man whose company held that
    contract was Ray Wallace. When Wallace died in 2002, his family came forward with the wooden
    feet he had used to stamp those first "Bigfoot" tracks into the mud. The brand name of the modern
    legend was, at its root, a joke that got out of hand.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Two creatures share one body here. One is a figure in the deep tradition of the Coast Salish
    peoples, held with respect for centuries. The other is a mid-century media sensation built on
    casts, film, and merchandise. Keep them separate, and the story gets clearer, not less.
  </div>
  <p>
    What makes Bigfoot so durable is the setting. The temperate rainforests of the Pacific Northwest
    are vast, dense, and genuinely under-travelled — country where a large animal really could go
    unseen, and where a frightened or hopeful mind fills the gaps between the trees. The habitat is
    real. The question has always been whether anything new lives in it.
  </p>
</Section>

  <p>
    The witnesses are strikingly consistent on the broad strokes. Bigfoot is described as a bipedal,
    ape-like figure roughly seven to nine feet tall, heavily built, covered in dark brown or reddish
    hair, with long arms, a crested or conical head set low on the shoulders, and a fluid upright
    walk that observers insist does not look like a person in a costume. A powerful, lingering stench
    — "like a wet dog and rotten eggs" — recurs across accounts often enough to have its own regional
    nickname, the Skunk Ape, in the American South.
  </p>
  <p>
    What varies is everything a hoaxer or a tired brain would get wrong: the exact height, the colour,
    the face, the eyeshine. And what is always missing is the one thing that would settle it — a
    carcass, a skeleton, a tooth, a confirmed strand of hair. For an animal supposedly this large,
    this widespread, and this long-reported, that absence is the single most important fact on the
    page.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      Before you decide what you are looking at, run the ordinary suspects first. A black bear
      standing or walking briefly on its hind legs is the single most common source of credible
      Bigfoot sightings, and in poor light at distance it is genuinely convincing. Elk, hikers in
      dark clothing, stumps, and shadow all pad out the record. The burden is on the extraordinary
      claim — not on the bear.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The reported pattern is not predation — it is avoidance. Unlike most entries in this guide,
    Bigfoot is almost never said to hunt people. The overwhelming majority of accounts describe a
    creature that flees, hides, or watches from cover, active mostly at dawn, dusk, and night, keeping
    to dense timber and water courses, and vanishing the instant it is noticed.
  </p>
  <p>
    The "evidence" it supposedly leaves is a familiar catalogue: lines of oversized footprints,
    twisted or stacked branches, tree knocks, whoops and howls in the dark, and the smell. Each of
    these is also something the forest, an ordinary animal, or a person with a motive can produce.
    That is the central difficulty — every trace Bigfoot leaves is a trace something known could leave
    too.
  </p>
  <p>
    The genuinely dangerous version of a Bigfoot encounter is almost always the mundane one. People
    get hurt in this country by going deeper and staying later than they planned because they are
    chasing a sound, by startling an actual bear, or by losing the light and the trail at once. The
    legend's real hazard is the way it pulls you off the path.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    This is the entry where honesty costs the monster the most, so here it is plainly: for all the
    volume of reports, Bigfoot has never produced a specimen that survives examination.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The tracks collapse.</strong> The footprints that built the modern legend at Bluff Creek
    were made with carved wooden feet, a fact Ray Wallace's own family disclosed after his death.
    Tracks are evidence of a track-maker, and track-makers can be human.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The film is unresolved — and that is not proof.</strong> The 1967 Patterson–Gimlin
    footage has resisted sixty years of analysis without being conclusively debunked, but "no one has
    proven it fake" is not the same as "it is real." One man says he wore the suit; the footage alone
    cannot settle it either way.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The DNA collapses.</strong> When hair samples attributed to Bigfoot and the Yeti were put
    through rigorous genetic sequencing in Bryan Sykes's 2014 study, every one resolved to a known
    animal — bears, cows, horses, a dog, a human. Not a single sample pointed to an unknown primate.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of this proves a negative — the forest is large and absence of evidence is not evidence of
    absence. But after decades of looking, the thing to prepare for is the wilderness itself, and the
    thing to be sceptical of is the specimen that never makes it to the lab.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two cases bookend the modern Bigfoot story: the piece of film that made it famous, and the
    genetic testing that tried, decades later, to finally pin it down.
  </p>

    <p>
      On October 20, 1967, Roger Patterson and Bob Gimlin were on horseback near Bluff Creek, a
      tributary of the Klamath River in Northern California, when — by their account — they filmed a
      large, dark, hair-covered figure walking upright along the creekbed. The roughly one-minute
      16mm clip, showing the subject striding away and turning to look back at the camera, became and
      remains the single most reproduced piece of Bigfoot evidence ever recorded.
    </p>
    <p>
      In the decades since, the film has been picked over by biomechanics experts, special-effects
      artists, and forensic analysts without a definitive verdict either way: no one has conclusively
      proven it a hoax, and no one has proven it genuine. A man named Bob Heironimus has publicly
      claimed he was the person inside an ape suit. The footage endures precisely because it sits in
      that unresolved gap — which, honestly read, is a reason for doubt, not proof of a creature.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>
  <EncounterCard
    id="Encounter 02 · Oxford, United Kingdom · 2014"

    <p>
      In 2014, Oxford geneticist Bryan Sykes and colleagues published a study in the Proceedings of
      the Royal Society B that did what eyewitnesses never can: it put the physical evidence under a
      sequencer. The team collected hair samples from around the world attributed to the Yeti,
      Bigfoot, and other "anomalous primates," decontaminated them, and sequenced their mitochondrial
      DNA.
    </p>
    <p>
      The result was the one the pattern predicts. Every sample matched a known animal — bears, a
      cow, horses, a dog, a porcupine, a tapir, and, in one case, a human. Not one came back as an
      unknown primate. Two Himalayan samples carried an intriguing genetic signature close to an
      ancient polar bear, hinting at an unusual bear rather than an ape-man. Sykes was careful to note
      that this does not prove such creatures cannot exist — but the hair that was supposed to be
      Bigfoot turned out, every time, to belong to something already in the textbooks.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      Bigfoot is the most-seen and least-proven creature in this guide, and both halves of that
      sentence are worth respecting. Generations of people across North America have come back from
      the trees certain of what they witnessed. And generation after generation, the carved feet, the
      disputed film, and the sequenced hair have pointed the same way — toward us, and toward the
      ordinary animals we already know.
    </p>
    <p>
      So go into the deep woods prepared for the real dangers: the cold, the drop, the bear, the lost
      trail, the early dark. Watch the ground, photograph what you find, and bag the sample for the
      lab. And if a tall shape ever stands at the edge of your light and then is gone —{' '}
      <em>back away slowly, and keep the original file.</em>
    </p>
    <p>End of journal entry. Mind the trail. Stay sceptical. The forest keeps its secrets, and most of them are just the forest.</p>
  </Outro>
