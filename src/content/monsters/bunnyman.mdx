<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is around midnight in October 1970, and you are sitting in a parked car with your fiancée at
    the edge of a field off Guinea Road, in the part of Fairfax County that is about to stop being
    countryside. This land is earmarked for a new housing development. You are talking, and the
    engine is quiet.
  </p>
  <p>
    Then someone is outside the car. He is shouting that you are on private property and that he has
    your tag number. He is wearing something white over his head — later, the papers will say a
    bunny suit — and he has a hatchet. The passenger window bursts inward. By the time you
    understand what has happened, he is gone into the dark, and the hatchet is lying in the car.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the forty-second journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the
    Bunnyman of Fairfax County, Virginia — a man in a costume who was reported to the police twice
    in one fortnight, and a legend that has spent half a century turning him into something he never
    was.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The Bunnyman is unusual in this guide because his beginning can be dated almost to the hour.
    According to Fairfax County Public Library historian-archivist Brian A. Conley, who spent years
    tracing the story, the first incident took place on 18 October 1970 on the 5400 block of Guinea
    Road, near Burke. A second followed on 29 October, about a block away, at a house under
    construction in the new Kings Park West development. The Washington Post covered both, under
    headlines that read like a children's book: "Man in Bunny Suit Sought in Fairfax" and "The
    'Rabbit' Reappears."
  </p>
  <p>
    That is the documented core. Everything else came later. By 1973 a University of Maryland
    student, Patricia Johnson, had collected 54 variations of the tale from fellow students for the
    Maryland Folklore Archive; according to Conley's summary, only three of them mentioned a murder.
    Somewhere in the retelling the Bunnyman left Guinea Road and moved roughly eight miles away, to
    a railway overpass on Colchester Road near Fairfax Station and Clifton — the place now marked on
    maps as Bunny Man Bridge.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The Bunnyman did not begin at the bridge. He began on the edge of a building site, with a
    hatchet and a grievance about trespassers. The bridge was added afterwards because it looked the
    part.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The version most people now know — an escaped asylum inmate in 1904, convicts on the run, dead
      rabbits at the bridge, teenagers murdered there on Halloween — was set down in a story
      credited to a Timothy Forbes and posted on the ghost-story website Castle of Spirits around
      1999. It claimed to draw on records in the Clifton Town Library. Conley checked each claim and
      reported that no such library has existed, that Fairfax County has never had an asylum for the
      insane, that Lorton Prison belonged to Washington, D.C.'s system and did not open until 1910,
      and that the named convicts appear in no county court record. Treat that post as what it is: a
      web claim from around 1999, not a source.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="What You Are Looking At">
  <StatRow

      { num: '0', label: 'Murders on record connected to the man or the bridge' },

  <p>
    What the 1970 witnesses saw was a man. The police went looking for someone in his late teens or
    early twenties, according to the Washingtonian's account of the case file. He carried a
    wooden-handled hatchet on the first night and an axe on the second. He spoke, in plain English,
    about property and trespassing. He ran away when challenged.
  </p>
  <p>
    The costume is where the record frays. The Post's headline put him in a bunny suit, and one
    later retelling describes white fur with long ears. But Conley found that the Fairfax Globe's
    report of the first incident described only a white covering over the head — perhaps a
    pillowcase, perhaps a hood — and he has suggested the full bunny suit may have been added by
    later tellers. Accounts differ, and you should let them.
  </p>
  <p>
    The bridge, for its part, is a stone railway underpass with a single lane of road running
    through it, set in woods off a dead-end stretch of Colchester Road. Visitors describe faded
    yellow paint and signs warning against trespassing and parking.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      The two places in this story are not the same place. Every documented incident happened on
      Guinea Road, among new houses. Nothing in the 1970 police record happened at the Colchester
      Overpass. If someone tells you the Bunnyman was seen at the bridge, they are telling you the
      legend, not the case.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The real Bunnyman had a pattern, and it was not supernatural. Both times, he appeared at night
    on land being turned from fields into a housing development. Both times, he confronted people he
    seemed to regard as intruders. Both times, he damaged something — a car window, a porch post —
    and then fled before anyone could catch him. Nobody was hurt.
  </p>
  <p>
    Conley's reading, after years with the papers and the people involved, is that the man was a
    local resident angry about the speed at which the area was being built over. He has said he
    cannot explain the costume. The police never identified anyone, and on 14 March 1971 the
    investigator closed the case as inactive.
  </p>
  <p>
    The legendary Bunnyman operates very differently. He haunts the overpass, appears on Halloween
    night, and kills whoever is still under the bridge at midnight. That version has no victims, no
    dates and no records behind it. It does, however, have a reliable effect: every October it sends
    cars full of people down a narrow lane to stand in a dark tunnel and wait. That is the behaviour
    worth surviving.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>There is no creature here, so the honest assessment concerns the story.</p>
  <p>
    <strong>The documented case is small.</strong> Two incidents, one hatchet, one damaged porch
    post, no injuries, no arrest. That is enough to frighten a neighbourhood. It is not enough to
    support a body count.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The legend fails on its own details.</strong> The asylum, the 1904 escape, the named
    convicts and the library archive were each checked by the county's own archivist and none held
    up. Conley also looked at the area's real unsolved and solved killings and found none connected
    to the bridge.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The bridge is borrowed scenery.</strong> It sits miles from where the reports were made.
    It was chosen, as far as anyone can tell, because it was secluded, eerie and already a place
    where teenagers went at night.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of that makes the 1970 witnesses unreliable. They reported what happened, the police wrote
    it down, and the papers printed it. The weakness lies in what was built on top of them.
  </p>
</Section>

  </p>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    The first case is the fortnight in 1970 that actually happened. The second is what Fairfax
    County has had to do about the legend since — at the bridge, every Halloween.

    <p>
      On 18 October 1970, Air Force Academy cadet Robert Bennett and his fiancée were parked near
      his uncle's house on the 5400 block of Guinea Road when a figure in white approached the car.
      By one account he shouted, "You're on private property, and I have your tag number," then
      threw a wooden-handled hatchet through the window. Neither was hurt. Decades later the couple
      confirmed the incident to Conley, and reportedly still had the hatchet.
    </p>
    <p>
      On the night of 29 October, Paul Phillips, a security guard on the Kings Park West
      construction, found a man on the porch of an unoccupied new house at 5307 Guinea Road. "I
      started talking to him and that's when he started chopping," Phillips was quoted as saying;
      the man swung an axe at a roof support, threatened him, and vanished into the trees. Six
      officers responded and found no one. Further calls followed — one from a man calling himself
      the "Axe Man" — but a stakeout produced nothing, and the case was closed as inactive in March
      1971.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      By 2003 the legend had become a traffic problem. Ahead of Halloween that year, Fairfax County
      Police announced extra foot, bicycle and vehicle patrols near the bridge, and blocked
      Colchester Road to visitors' cars at Fairfax Station Road from 6 p.m. to midnight. People
      could walk in; only residents could drive on. Officers said they would enforce parking,
      trespassing and public drinking laws, and neighbours had put up hand-painted signs to keep
      visitors out of their driveways.
    </p>
    <p>
      The visitors came anyway. One driver that night had brought a carload of passengers after a
      television countdown of scary places claimed 21 people had been killed near the bridge;
      finding nothing, they left disappointed. A police sergeant summed it up: "It's Fairfax
      County's 'Blair Witch Project.'" In 2017, presenting his research at a county library, Conley
      offered his own verdict on the story's staying power: "It will not die."
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Bunnyman is the rare monster whose case file you can actually read. In October 1970 a man
      in a white costume frightened a young couple and a night watchman on a building site, broke a
      car window and gouged a porch post, and was never caught. That is the whole of the record.
    </p>
    <p>
      The rest — the asylum, the convict, the rabbits on the bridge, the bodies on Halloween — was
      built later, by teenagers, tellers and at least one web post, and pinned to a stone tunnel
      miles away because it looked like the kind of place where such a thing should happen. The
      murders never happened. The crowds did.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Read the police report before the legend. Walk, don't park. And stay out
      of the tunnel when the headlights come.
    </p>
  </Outro>
