<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is a morning in the hill country outside Canóvanas, Puerto Rico, and a farmer is standing
    over eight dead goats. There is no blood on the ground. The bodies are intact — no sign that
    anything tried to eat them — but each one has two or three small, deep punctures in the neck.
    Nothing in the farmer's experience leaves a kill like this and then walks away hungry.
  </p>
  <p>
    Word travels. By nightfall a neighbour has lost chickens the same way. Within weeks the island
    has a name for the thing doing it, and within a year that name has jumped to Mexico, to Texas,
    to the evening news. It is one of the few monsters in this guide young enough that we can watch
    it being born — and that, as it turns out, is exactly the problem.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the twelfth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject: the Chupacabra —
    the goat-sucker. It is also the entry where the guide has to be most honest with you, because
    the most useful thing we can tell you about this creature is how a monster gets made.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The Chupacabra has a birthday, which is rare. In August 1995, in the municipality of Canóvanas,
    farmers began reporting livestock killed in a strange way — puncture wounds, no obvious feeding,
    the animals apparently drained. A resident named Madelyne Tolentino gave the account that
    defined everything to follow: a creature standing on two legs, three to four feet tall, with
    huge dark eyes, thin arms, and a row of spines running down its back.
  </p>
  <p>
    The name came shortly after. The Puerto Rican comedian and television personality Silverio Pérez
    is generally credited with coining <em>el chupacabras</em> — literally "the goat-sucker" — and
    the label was too good not to spread. Within months the creature had crossed the water to the
    Latin American mainland, carried by newspapers and the young internet, each retelling adding
    teeth, spines, and glowing eyes.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Most monsters are old and anonymous. This one is young enough that we can name the first
    witness, the week it appeared, and the movie it looked like. That is not a weakness in the
    story. It is the story.
  </div>
  <p>
    In his five-year investigation, published as <em>Tracking the Chupacabra</em> (2011), folklorist
    Benjamin Radford traced that founding description back to a specific source. Tolentino, he
    found, had recently seen the 1995 science-fiction film <em>Species</em> — whose alien creature,
    Sil, closely resembles the spined, big-eyed biped she described. Radford concluded that "the
    most important chupacabra description cannot be trusted." The monster, in other words, may have
    walked out of a cinema before it walked into a goat pen.
  </p>
</Section>

  <p>
    There is no single Chupacabra. There are two, and they do not match. The original — the Puerto
    Rican version — is the bipedal, reptilian, spiny-backed creature of Tolentino's account, roughly
    the size of a small person, with oversized eyes. It is described, not recovered. No specimen of
    it has ever been produced.
  </p>
  <p>
    The second arrived later, in Mexico and the US Southwest: a four-legged animal, hairless, grey,
    with a pronounced spine, leathery skin, and a long muzzle full of prominent teeth. This one does
    get recovered — ranchers shoot it, photograph it, freeze it. It is real, physical, and testable.
    It is also, every single time, a dog family animal with mange.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      When someone shows you "a chupacabra," establish which one they mean. If it is the upright
      spined creature, you are looking at a story. If it is the hairless four-legged animal, you are
      looking at a sick coyote or dog — and sarcoptic mange can make a familiar animal genuinely
      unrecognisable, shrinking it, greying the skin, and giving it the gaunt, wrong silhouette that
      sells the legend.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The reported pattern is consistent: livestock — goats, chickens, sheep — found dead overnight,
    intact, with small puncture wounds and, supposedly, no blood. The "no blood" claim is the engine
    of the whole myth, the detail that turns an ordinary predator kill into something unnatural.
  </p>
  <p>
    It is also the detail that failed first. When attacked animals were actually given a necropsy,
    they were found to have normal blood levels. "They were not, in fact, vampirized," Radford noted
    of the examined carcasses. Blood settles and clots inside a body after death; a predator that
    bites, kills, and is then interrupted or simply not hungry will leave exactly the scene that
    launched a legend — a punctured, uneaten animal that only looks drained.
  </p>
  <p>
    What is real here is livestock loss, and it is not trivial to a farmer who depends on the herd.
    But the culprits are the ones that have always taken livestock at night: coyotes, feral and
    stray dogs, and other everyday predators, doing everyday damage that a strange name made
    strange.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    This is the entry where the honest assessment is the whole point, so here it is plainly: the
    Chupacabra has never survived contact with a laboratory.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The description collapses.</strong> The founding Puerto Rican account traces to a film,
    and the creature's appearance has drifted freely ever since — a sign of a story being retold,
    not an animal being observed.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The carcasses collapse.</strong> Every physical "chupacabra" that has been examined and
    genetically tested has come back as a coyote, a dog, or a coyote-dog hybrid, typically ravaged
    by mange. Not one has been an unknown species.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The signature collapses.</strong> The "bloodless kill" — the one feature that made this
    more than a predator problem — does not hold up to a necropsy.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of that makes the fear unreal, or the dead goats imaginary. It means the thing to defend
    against is ordinary, and the thing to be sceptical of is the story.
  </p>
</Section>

    <p>
      In August 1995, after a wave of strangely punctured, apparently bloodless livestock killings
      around Canóvanas, resident Madelyne Tolentino described seeing the animal responsible: an
      upright creature, three to four feet tall, with large dark eyes, thin limbs, and spines down
      its back. Her account, widely repeated in the Puerto Rican press, became the template for
      every Chupacabra that followed.
    </p>
    <p>
      Years later, folklorist Benjamin Radford interviewed Tolentino as part of a five-year
      investigation and found that she had recently watched the 1995 film <em>Species</em>, whose
      creature — a spined, large-eyed humanoid — closely matched her description. Radford concluded
      that the single most influential eyewitness account of the Chupacabra could not be relied
      upon, and that the creature's defining image may have originated on screen.
    </p>

      In 2007, rancher Phylis Canion of Cuero, Texas, recovered the bodies of several strange,
      hairless, grey animals she associated with the deaths of dozens of her chickens. She kept a
      head in her freezer and, unlike almost every earlier claimant, pursued laboratory testing
      rather than publicity alone.
    </p>
    <p>
      The result was the one the pattern predicts. DNA analysis identified the animal as a member of
      the dog family — the "Texas chupacabra," like every other recovered specimen, turning out to
      be a coyote or coyote-dog hybrid, its uncanny appearance the work of sarcoptic mange rather
      than a new branch of the tree of life. The specimen that was supposed to prove the creature
      instead became the clearest evidence against it.
