<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is somewhere past the hour a small child should be asleep, and the small child is not asleep.
    The room is dark except for a candle or a hallway light. A parent — tired, out of patience, out
    of options — leans into the doorway and says the oldest thing in the book. Go to sleep. If you
    do not go to sleep, <em>el Coco</em> will come.
  </p>
  <p>
    The child does not ask what el Coco looks like, because el Coco does not look like anything.
    That is the whole trick. He is a shape at the edge of the room, a weight on the roof, a thing
    that takes children who will not close their eyes. He has no face, and so he wears the one the
    child is most afraid of.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the twenty-eighth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide, and it is a strange one,
    because the subject is not a creature you can meet in the woods. El Coco lives in doorways and
    lullabies. He has frightened children across the Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking world for
    centuries — and the most useful thing this guide can tell you is who actually sends him.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    El Coco is one of the oldest bogeymen on record in the Iberian world. Folklorists trace the
    figure to northern Portugal and Galicia, where the words <em>coco</em> and <em>coca</em> mean,
    roughly, "head" or "skull" — and where a related female figure, the Coca, appears in old
    processional traditions. From the peninsula the figure travelled on the tongues of parents: with
    Spanish and Portuguese colonisation it spread across Latin America as el Cuco, el Cucuy, and la
    Cuca, and as far as the Philippines.
  </p>
  <p>
    The oldest surviving rhyme about him dates to the 17th century, preserved in a dramatic
    <em> auto</em> by the playwright Juan Caxés. But the figure was never really a story with a
    plot. He was a tool — a word you reached for when a child would not sleep and reason had failed.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Most monsters in this guide are described in detail. El Coco is defined by the opposite: he is
    whatever shape a child's fear needs him to be. The vagueness is not a gap in the record. It is
    the design.
  </div>
  <p>
    That is why there is no definitive portrait of him. In some regions he is a shrouded figure, in
    others a lurking animal, in Brazil a fearsome alligator-woman named Cuca. The constant across
    every version is not the body but the sentence: <em>behave, or he comes for you.</em>
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The Coco belongs to a family of child-frightening figures that appears in nearly every culture
      — the English bogeyman, the Slavic Baba Yaga, the sack-man of a dozen countries. They are
      among the most universal of folk inventions precisely because they answer a universal problem:
      a tired adult and a child who will not obey. The monster is imaginary. The bedtime standoff is
      very real.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    There is nothing consistent to see, and that is the first thing to understand about him. Ask ten
    adults who were raised on el Coco what he looks like and you will get ten different answers — a
    hunched shadow, a man in a sack, a hairy thing, a pair of eyes under the bed. None of them is
    wrong, because the figure was never standardised. He is a placeholder for dread.
  </p>
  <p>
    What is consistent is the <em>function</em>. El Coco is invoked, almost always, at two moments —
    when a child will not sleep, and when a child will not behave. He is a lever, pulled by a
    parent, and the shape he takes is supplied by the child's own imagination. That is what makes
    him so effective and so hard to fight. You cannot kill a thing that lives in the listener's
    head.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      If a child reports el Coco, do not go looking for el Coco. Look for what the child is actually
      frightened of — the dark, the empty hallway, being alone, the adult who named him. The figure
      is a container for a real fear, and treating the container as the problem leaves the fear
      exactly where it was.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    El Coco does not hunt. He is summoned. The mechanism is a sentence, usually a lullaby or a
    warning, and it works by attaching an enormous, formless consequence to a small act of
    disobedience. The child cannot picture the punishment clearly, so the child pictures the worst
    thing they can — and complies.
  </p>
  <p>
    The most famous delivery system is the cradle song.{' '}
    <em>Duérmete niño, duérmete ya, que viene el Coco y te comerá</em> — "sleep, child, sleep now,
    or the Coco comes and will eat you." It is sung in the gentle register of a lullaby while
    carrying, in plain words, a threat of being devoured. That contradiction — a soothing melody
    wrapped around a warning — is the entire machinery of el Coco in miniature.
  </p>
  <p>
    It is also self-limiting. The threat works on the very young and loses its grip as the child
    grows old enough to doubt it. There is no documented pattern of el Coco "appearing" to anyone,
    because there is nothing to appear. What persists is the memory of the fear, and for some, the
    uneasy inheritance of passing the same words down the line.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    This is the easiest honest assessment in the guide, because el Coco has no body to find a
    weakness in. He is not a creature that can be fought, trapped, or verified. He is a parenting
    instrument, and the honest questions are about the instrument, not the monster.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>He dissolves under explanation.</strong> Unlike a predator, the Coco cannot survive a
    child understanding what he is. The moment an adult explains that he is a story told to
    encourage sleep, the figure has nothing left to stand on.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>He dies with age.</strong> The threat is calibrated to the credulity of the very young.
    Pull the lever on an older child and it does nothing — or worse, it breeds resentment and the
    sense of having been manipulated.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>And the method itself is now questioned.</strong> Fear-based discipline can buy
    compliance in the short term, but child-development research broadly favours calm, consistent
    guidance over scare tactics, which tend to raise anxiety without teaching the behaviour they
    were meant to. Even in Goya's day, as the next section shows, the tactic had its critics.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two cases are not sightings — el Coco leaves no carcass and no witness. They are
    documented pieces of cultural history: the lullaby that carried him for centuries, and the
    moment one of the greatest artists in Europe put the whole mechanism on a single plate.
  </p>

    <p>
      The most durable record of el Coco is not a painting or a chronicle but a song that parents
      still sing. The traditional cradle rhyme —{' '}
      <em>Duérmete niño, duérmete ya, que viene el Coco y te comerá</em> — instructs the child to
      sleep at once, because the Coco is coming and will eat them. It is sung in the soft cadence of
      any lullaby, which is exactly what makes it remarkable: a threat of being devoured, delivered
      as a comfort.
    </p>
    <p>
      The oldest surviving verse of this kind is traced to the 17th century, preserved in a dramatic
      <em> auto</em> by the Spanish playwright Juan Caxés. From that root the figure travelled
      wherever Spanish and Portuguese were spoken — becoming el Cucuy in Mexico, el Cuco across much
      of Latin America, and the Coca in the Portuguese tradition — carried not by books but by
      caregivers repeating the same few lines to the next sleepless child. It is one of the clearest
      examples we have of folklore surviving purely as a tool of everyday parenting.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      In 1799 Francisco Goya published <em>Los Caprichos</em>, a series of etchings satirising the
      follies of Spanish society. The third plate is titled <em>Que viene el Coco</em> — "Here comes
      the bogeyman." It shows a mother and two small children recoiling as a tall, shrouded,
      faceless figure looms over them in the dark. It is el Coco, rendered by one of the most
      important artists in European history, and the image is not flattering to the people who
      summon him.
    </p>
    <p>
      Museum scholarship reads the plate as a criticism of a fear-based upbringing. The Metropolitan
      Museum of Art describes it as Goya's attack on an educational system built on frightening
      children with supernatural beings, and notes the darker possibility that the "bogeyman" —
      wearing fashionable pointed shoes beneath his robe — is the mother's lover in disguise. The
      Seattle Art Museum frames the same image as propaganda for Enlightenment reforms that warned
      against using scare tactics on children. Either way, the verdict is the one this entry keeps
      arriving at — the thing to be wary of is not the Coco, but the adult holding him.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      El Coco is the rare monster in this guide that was never meant to be real. He is a word for a
      consequence too large to picture, handed to a child in the dark so the child will close their
      eyes. He has no shape because he does not need one. We supply the shape ourselves, and then we
      pass it on.
    </p>
    <p>
      Goya saw the whole machine two centuries ago and put it on a plate — the looming figure, the
      terrified children, and the grown-up who called him into the room. That is still the honest
      picture. The danger here was never the bogeyman. It was the ease of reaching for him.
    </p>
    <p>
      So when the room is dark and the child will not sleep and the oldest sentence in the book is
      right there on your tongue — <em>name the fear instead of feeding it.</em>
    </p>
    <p>End of journal entry. Mind the doorway. Stay sceptical. The monster was us all along.</p>
  </Outro>
