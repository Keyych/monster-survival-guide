<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is a little after half past midnight in November 2007, somewhere in south Fresno, and you are
    asleep. Outside, above the garage, a security camera you put up only recently is doing its job:
    a fixed, grey, low-resolution view of your own front yard, the lawn flat and colourless under
    whatever light reaches it. Nothing moves. Then something does.
  </p>
  <p>
    It walks in from the edge of the frame — pale, small, and wrong. There is no body to speak of,
    no arms, no obvious head; just two long white legs, joined at the top, stepping across the grass
    with a stiff, jointed rhythm, as though someone were working each leg on a string. A few minutes
    later a second one follows, smaller than the first. You will not see any of this until the next
    day, when you play the tape back.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the forty-first journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the Fresno
    Nightcrawlers — a creature that exists, as far as anyone can show, only on two short pieces of
    security footage. It is a case about video, not about a monster, and it is worth your attention
    for one reason above all: the footage predates the age of AI-generated video, and it still
    cannot be traced.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The Fresno Nightcrawlers have no folklore behind them. There is no generations-old warning and
    no regional tradition — only a home-security tape, a television segment, and an internet that
    found the figures funny, eerie and strangely endearing all at once. Where most monsters arrive
    wrapped in centuries of story, this one arrived as a clip of about twenty seconds.
  </p>
  <p>
    The accepted origin runs like this. A south Fresno resident who has only ever been identified by
    his first name, Jose, set up a camera outside his house — to find out why his dogs were barking
    at night, by one account, or because of burglaries in the area, by another. The footage is dated
    to the night of 5 November 2007. Because his system reportedly overwrote recordings on playback,
    he filmed the monitor with a separate video camera, and that copy is what survives. He took the
    tape to the Fresno office of the Spanish-language network Univision, which brought in Victor
    Camacho, host of the late-night paranormal radio show <em>Los Desvelados</em>. Within a
    fortnight, a video examining the footage had appeared on Camacho's YouTube channel.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    This creature has a name because a television programme gave it one. It is not a word handed
    down through a culture; it is a label applied three years after the footage, for broadcast. Keep
    that order of events in mind — it is the whole shape of this case.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The name "Fresno Nightcrawler" was coined in October 2010 by Syfy's{' '}
      <em>Fact or Faked: Paranormal Files</em>, which ran the clip in its second episode. That
      exposure carried the figures well beyond local Spanish-language media and into
      English-language blogs, list sites and forums. Claims that the creatures belong to a local
      Native American legend circulate widely online, but none of the versions this guide found
      names a community or a source; Michael Banti of the blog <em>Weird Fresno</em> reportedly
      asked regional tribes about images being passed off as tribal carvings of the creature, and
      his contacts did not recognise them. Treat the "ancient legend" framing as internet
      decoration, not tradition.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="What You Are Looking At">

  <p>
    The figures are consistent across both clips in the broad shape. Each is a small, pale, roughly
    white form that appears to be all legs — two long limbs joined at a rounded top, with no visible
    arms or face. They walk upright, one leg after the other, in a stiff and rhythmic gait that
    writer Brian Dunning likened to a marionette. In the Fresno clip a second, smaller figure
    follows the first a few minutes later; in the 2011 clip two figures appear together, one taller
    than the other.
  </p>
  <p>
    The size is where it frays. Camacho, after visiting the yard in February 2008, has been reported
    as estimating the figures at no more than half a metre in one telling and at two to four feet in
    another. In the 2011 clip, the man who posted it superimposed a daytime photograph of the path
    with a baby stroller placed in the frame, putting the taller figure at about stroller height.
    That is a careful-looking piece of scale work — done by the uploader, on footage nobody else has
    been able to examine in its original form.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      "Before AI" is not the same as "unfakeable". In 2007 a convincing small walking figure on a
      grainy night-vision camera could be made with a puppet, a costume on a stick, stilts, or
      ordinary editing software — and a copy filmed off a monitor hides the seams of all of them.
      The age of this footage removes one modern suspect. It does not clear the older ones.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    There is almost nothing to operate. The Fresno Nightcrawlers do not approach anyone, do not
    attack, and do not react to the camera. In both clips they simply walk across the frame and out
    of it. No sound was recorded, no one reported seeing them with their own eyes, and nothing was
    found afterwards that has been independently verified.
  </p>
  <p>
    What the case does have is a pattern of transmission, and that is the honest description of its
    "behaviour". A private tape goes to a local broadcaster; a paranormal radio host takes it up and
    presents it at the 2008 MUFON UFO Symposium in San Jose; a national television show names it and
    declares it genuine; a second clip turns up on YouTube; and from there the figures pass into
    list articles, podcasts and plush toys. Each step adds reach. None adds evidence.
  </p>
  <p>
    The trail has also gone cold at the source. According to Camacho, as reported in 2018, Jose died
    some years after the recording. The man who handled the original tape can no longer be asked
    about it, and no original file has ever been made public.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    This is a case with no monster to fight, so the honest assessment is about the evidence rather
    than the creature.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The provenance is close to nothing.</strong> Both clips come from people who were never
    publicly named. The Fresno footage is a camera recording of a screen; the Yosemite clip arrived
    through a self-described UFO enthusiast who said friends had sent it. Even its location is
    disputed — some accounts put it inside Yosemite National Park, others in the residential
    community of Yosemite Lakes Park to the south, and some say park staff filmed it, which the
    uploader's own account does not.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The ordinary explanations fit.</strong> The commonest sceptical reading is a hoax: a
    puppet or small costume walked across a lawn, or a figure created with camera tricks. According
    to <em>The Ghost in My Machine</em>, the YouTube effects debunker Captain Disillusion
    demonstrated in 2012 that footage of this kind could be produced that way. None of this proves
    how these clips were made, but each explanation needs far less than an unknown animal.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The strongest point is also the weakest.</strong> <em>Fact or Faked</em> reportedly
    could not recreate the footage and pronounced it authentic. But a production team failing to
    match a clip is a statement about that team, not about the clip — and a MUFON look at the
    footage, as summarised on a National Park Service blog in 2023, came back simply "inconclusive".
  </p>
  <p>
    None of that calls anyone a liar. Dunning, writing in 2022, declined even to argue authenticity:
    he judged that the creature has little real belief behind it and set out to explain why it is so
    popular instead. That is a fair summary of where the case sits — loved, not believed.
  </p>
</Section>

    <p>
      The footage is dated to the night of 5 November 2007, with the first figure appearing just
      after 12:40am and the second about five minutes later. The homeowner, identified only as Jose,
      recorded the playback on a separate camera and took the tape to Univision's Fresno office.
      Univision asked radio host Victor Camacho to say on air that the figures were aliens; by
      Dunning's account Camacho declined, saying he did not know what they were. Camacho posted a
      video about the footage on 19 November 2007, visited the yard in February 2008, and presented
      the case at the MUFON UFO Symposium in San Jose later that year.
    </p>
    <p>
      In October 2010 the clip appeared on Syfy's <em>Fact or Faked: Paranormal Files</em>, which
      named the creature and declared the footage genuine. By 2018 it was circulating widely enough
      that Gordon Bonnet could write up a list-site feature on it, noting that the case for
      authenticity was secondhand and that digital editing made claims that it was impossible to
      fake doubtful. No original file has been released, and Jose's identity was never made public.
    </p>

    <p>
      In April 2011 a YouTuber posting as Dovus X-Life Operior, a self-described fan of UFOs and the
      paranormal, uploaded security footage he said had been recorded the previous month by retired
      friends. The clip is dated 28 March 2011, at around 2:18am, and shows two pale, legs-only
      figures on a paved path, one taller than the other. To show scale, he laid the video over a
      daytime photograph of the same path with a baby stroller in shot. Dunning notes, drily, how
      convenient it was that the anonymous witnesses happened to contact a paranormal enthusiast.
    </p>
    <p>
      The retellings disagree about almost everything else. A 2015 article already described the
      clip as Yosemite National Park security footage filmed while staff hunted for vandals — and,
      in the same piece, dated the Fresno tape to the 1990s, which no other source supports. Later
      local reporting places the clip in Yosemite Lakes Park, a residential community outside the
      national park, and says it has reportedly been debunked. A National Park Service blog in 2023
      mentioned the Fresno and Yosemite figures among the area's oddities. Fifteen years on, nobody
      has produced the original recording or the people who made it.

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Fresno Nightcrawlers are the smallest entry in this guide and one of the most modern. For
      about twenty seconds in 2007, a camera above a garage recorded something it could not explain,
      and for three years afterwards that tape and one more passed from hand to hand until a
      television show gave them a name and the internet gave them a fan club.
    </p>
    <p>
      That is worth remembering now that anyone can make a walking pair of legs in seconds. These
      clips were made before that was possible, and they are still no more trustworthy for it —
      because a video is only ever as good as the chain of people who handled it, and here that
      chain is broken at the first link. The lesson was never about artificial intelligence. It was
      about provenance.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Ask who filmed it. Find the first upload. And stay sceptical — a clip
      that cannot be traced has already told you most of what it can.
    </p>
  </Outro>
