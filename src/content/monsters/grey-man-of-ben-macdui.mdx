<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    You are alone on the summit plateau of Ben Macdui, the second-highest mountain in Britain, and
    the cloud has come down. There is snow underfoot. The great cairn is somewhere behind you now,
    and the world has shrunk to a few yards of grey stone and grey air. The only sound is the crunch
    of your own boots.
  </p>
  <p>
    Then you are not sure it is the only sound. For every few steps you take, there is another
    crunch — heavier, slower, as if something behind you were walking in your tracks with a stride
    three or four times the length of yours. You stop. You look. There is nothing in the mist. You
    walk on, and it walks on too. You are an experienced mountaineer and you do not mind being alone
    on the hills. You are also, suddenly, more frightened than you have ever been.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the forty-fourth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the Grey
    Man of Ben Macdui — Am Fear Liath Mòr, the Big Grey Man — a presence reported by climbers on a
    high, cold plateau in the Cairngorms. Nobody has ever been hurt by it. That is not the same as
    saying nobody has been hurt by being afraid of it.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The Grey Man entered the written record at an after-dinner speech. In late November 1925 the
    Cairngorm Club, an Aberdeen mountaineering club founded in 1889, held its thirty-seventh annual
    meeting, followed by a dinner for over a hundred members and friends. Replying to the toast, the
    club's Honorary President, Professor J. Norman Collie — Fellow of the Royal Society, professor
    of organic chemistry in London, and a respected mountaineer — described an experience that had
    befallen him alone on Ben Macdui "over thirty-five years ago". Later writers date it to 1891.
  </p>
  <p>
    The club's journal printed the story in 1926, and it is worth reading what it actually says.
    Collie heard footsteps; he did not see anything. About twelve years later he told the story to
    Dr Kellas, a fellow mountaineer, who said he had once seen something odd at the summit one June
    midnight. And a Mr Colin Phillip reportedly heard from an old man at the edge of Rothiemurchus
    Forest that this would have been the "Ferla Mhor" — the Big Grey Man.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The Grey Man is not a creature anyone described first and named later. It is a feeling on a
    mountain, borrowed a Gaelic name, and only afterwards given a body by the people who retold it.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      When the speech reached the newspapers, the journal recorded "a flood of interviews and
      correspondence" that ran for weeks under headings such as "Ben Macdhui Ghost". Some writers
      put Collie's fright down to a fit of nerves. Others described their own uneasy feelings in
      mist, or mysterious music on the Cairngorms that turned out to be wind rising and falling in
      the gullies. The journal itself declined to judge, leaving the matter "to the judgment of the
      individual reader". Later retellings added height, grey fur and pointed ears; none of that is
      in the 1926 text.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    Most of the time, you are not looking at anything. The core of the Grey Man is sound and
    feeling: footsteps that seem to keep pace from behind at an impossible stride, and a sudden,
    overwhelming terror that the witness cannot account for. Collie said the fear seized him by the
    throat. Later accounts repeat the pattern — dread first, the urge to flee second, and only
    occasionally a shape.
  </p>
  <p>
    When a shape does appear, it is tall and indistinct. Kellas's figure, as Collie relayed it, was
    a man roughly the height of the summit cairn, which Collie put at ten feet or more — and
    Kellas's brother, sitting close by, said he saw no one at all. Popular retellings since have
    offered a hairy giant, a figure with talon-like feet, even one in a top hat. The more detailed
    the description, the further it tends to sit from any first-hand source.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      A huge grey figure standing in mist, keeping its distance and moving when you move, is also an
      exact description of a Brocken spectre: your own shadow, thrown by a low sun onto cloud,
      magnified and sometimes ringed with a halo of light. It needs only sun behind you and mist in
      front. Before you file a giant, move your arm and see whether it moves too.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The pattern in the classic accounts is consistent enough to describe. The witness is alone, or
    nearly so. They are on or near the summit plateau, usually in mist, cloud or failing light,
    often on snow or gravel. They become aware of footsteps out of step with their own. The fear
    arrives fast and out of proportion. And then they run — Collie for miles, Tewnion down to Glen
    Derry, others simply as far and as fast as they could from the top.
  </p>
  <p>
    Nothing in the record follows them down. There is no account of contact, no injury inflicted, no
    pursuit beyond the plateau. The phenomenon, if it is one, seems to live entirely in a few square
    miles of high ground, in bad visibility, in the minds of people who are tired, cold and far from
    help.
  </p>
  <p>
    That is also a precise description of the conditions in which human perception fails. The
    Cairngorm plateau is the highest, coldest and snowiest ground in the British Isles, broad and
    bouldery on top, with steep corries cutting into its edges. In a whiteout it is easy to lose any
    sense of direction or distance. Whatever the Grey Man is, it only ever shows itself where your
    senses are least reliable and your mistakes most expensive.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    There is no creature on record to resist, so this is an assessment of the evidence — and of the
    explanations, which are stronger than the monster.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The Brocken spectre explains the giant.</strong> The effect was described from the
    Brocken in Germany in 1780, and it has long been known in Scotland. The poet James Hogg, in
    1791, saw a towering figure in fog, took off his hat, watched the figure do the same, and
    concluded it was his own shadow. It explains a vast figure that keeps its distance. It does not
    explain footsteps.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Ordinary noise explains a good deal of the sound.</strong> On a frozen plateau, ice
    forms and cracks, stones split and fall, and deer, hares and ptarmigan move about unseen. Trail
    magazine has suggested refreezing footprints in snow could crunch behind a walker. In 1925
    itself, correspondents traced mysterious "music" on the Cairngorms to wind in the gullies.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Fear explains the rest.</strong> Exhaustion, isolation and cold make the mind misread
    small signals. Mountaineers have a name — mountain panic — for sudden, causeless dread in wild
    places, and it is reported far beyond Ben Macdui. Some writers have also suggested infrasound,
    very low-frequency sound from wind, as a cause of unease; that idea draws on laboratory work
    elsewhere and remains a suggestion rather than a finding on this mountain.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The source has a twinkle in it.</strong> Collie's biographer, Christine Mill, described
    a man with a lifelong interest in the occult and a gift for tales, and the outdoor writer
    Cameron McNeish has called him a well-known practical joker. None of that proves the story was
    invented. It does mean the founding text is an after-dinner anecdote, told decades after the
    fact.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Six Rules That May Save Your Life">
  <p>
    The Grey Man has never harmed anyone. The mountain has, and the record shows frightened people
    running blind across ground where running is the most dangerous thing you can do. These rules
    are ordinary Scottish mountain safety, which is the only real defence on Ben Macdui.
  </p>

    <p>
      As the Cairngorm Club Journal set it down, Collie was returning alone from the summit cairn in
      mist, on snow, when he began to hear something other than his own footsteps: a big crunch for
      every few of his steps, as though someone were following with a stride three or four times his
      own. He told himself it was nonsense, listened, heard it again, and saw nothing. Then the
      terror came. He ran, staggering blindly among the boulders, for four or five miles, nearly
      down to Rothiemurchus Forest.
    </p>
    <p>
      He added Kellas's story of a cairn-high figure seen at midnight that Kellas's brother never
      saw, and the old man's verdict that it was the Big Grey Man. Then he closed by saying there
      was something very queer about the top of Ben Macdui, and that he would not go back there
      alone. The journal reported the newspaper storm that followed, gave space to the sceptics, and
      left the verdict to its readers.
    </p>

    <p>
      In October 1943, during a ten-day climbing leave in the Cairngorms, Alexander Tewnion reached
      the summit of Ben Macdui alone as mist closed over the cairn. He was carrying a loaded
      revolver. By his own account, published fifteen years later in The Scots Magazine, he heard
      heavy footsteps and saw a shape loom out of the mist towards him. He fired three times and ran
      down to Glen Derry. Retellings quote his verdict both ways — as believing it was the Grey Man,
      and as concluding he had shot the only one his imagination would ever see.
    </p>
    <p>
      Later retellings also mention Peter Densham, who reportedly did aircraft rescue work in the
      Cairngorms during the war and fled the summit in panic in 1945; accounts of what he heard and
      felt differ, and the earliest source for them is unclear. What Tewnion's case shows most
      plainly is the real hazard of this entry: a frightened man firing into fog, on a summit where
      the most likely figure in the mist is another walker.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Grey Man of Ben Macdui began as footsteps in snow and a fear that a sensible man could not
      explain, told to a room of fellow climbers more than three decades later. Everything since —
      the fur, the height, the pointed ears — was added by people who were never on the summit with
      him.
    </p>
    <p>
      What remains true is the mountain. A broad, bouldery plateau, the cloud coming down, cliffs at
      the edges, and a mind that fills silence with company. The shadow in the mist may be your own.
      The crunch behind you may be ice. The fear is real, and it is the fear, not the Grey Man, that
      can walk you off an edge.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Carry the compass. Check your shadow. And whatever you hear behind you
      in the mist, do not run.
    </p>
  </Outro>
</Section>
