<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is a Sunday evening in late August 1955, still hot, and you are in a small farmhouse at
    Kelly, a scatter of homes about eight miles north of Hopkinsville, Kentucky. There is no
    telephone here. Eleven people are in or around the house — adults, visitors, three children —
    and one of the visitors has just come back from the well saying he saw a bright light cross the
    sky. Nobody takes him seriously.
  </p>
  <p>
    An hour later the dog starts barking. Two of the men go to look, and come back in fast for a
    rifle and a shotgun. For the next three hours they will fire at faces in the windows and at
    small shapes on the roof and in the trees, and nothing they hit will stay down. Around eleven
    o'clock everyone crams into two cars and drives to the police station in Hopkinsville.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the fortieth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is what the
    newspapers soon called the little men of Kelly, and what the rest of the world remembers as the
    Hopkinsville Goblins. It is one of the most famous close encounters on record. It is also a
    story about a dark yard, a frightened household, and a great deal of gunfire.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    There is no folklore behind the Kelly creatures. They arrived in 1955, at the height of the
    American flying-saucer craze, and they arrived as a police matter. The first press account, in
    the <em>Kentucky New Era</em> on 22 August, ran under a headline about a space ship and twelve
    little men, and described two carloads of people turning up at police headquarters around 11
    p.m. with a story of creatures that bullets could not stop.
  </p>
  <p>
    The response was serious. The paper reported more than a dozen city, county and state officers
    at the farm, along with four military police from Fort Campbell, the Army base nearby. They
    stayed until after 2 a.m. and found no tracks and no sign that anything had landed — only a hole
    in a window screen where the household said a shot had gone through. Officials offered no
    opinion, though the paper noted they agreed there had been no drinking.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The earliest accounts of Kelly did not call the creatures green. The colour came later, from the
    national press and the retellings, and it tied the case so firmly to an older phrase — "little
    green men" — that many people assume Kelly invented it. Keep that in mind: the most famous
    detail of this case is one the first reports never gave.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The most thorough early investigation was by the ufologist Isabel Davis, who went to Kelly in
      1956, a year after the event. Her work, written up with Ted Bloecher, was not published until
      1978, when the Center for UFO Studies issued it as{' '}
      <em>Close Encounter at Kelly and Others of 1955</em>. Davis and Bloecher describe the beings
      as silvery and metallic rather than green, and their report is the source that later
      investigators, sceptics included, still lean on.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    The first newspaper story described the visitors as about four feet tall, with big heads, huge
    eyes, long arms and oversized hands, and something like metal plating for clothing. By the time
    the household's mother, Glennie Lankford, signed a statement the next day, they had shrunk to
    around two and a half feet, with large pointed ears, clawed hands and eyes that shone yellow.
    Later descriptions add thin, stiff legs.
  </p>
  <p>
    The numbers drift too. The earliest report spoke of twelve to fifteen creatures. Yet when
    investigators went through the accounts, there was only one moment when anyone saw more than one
    at the same time — a pair, one on the roof and one in a tree. The rest of the night, as far as
    the record shows, may have been the same one or two figures seen again and again.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      Big yellow eyes, pointed "ears", no visible neck, talons, about two feet tall, perched on a
      roof or a branch and then seeming to float to the ground. Before you read that list as a
      visitor from space, read it again as a description of a bird that hunts at dusk in rural
      Kentucky.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    According to the accounts, the pattern was simple. A figure would appear — at a window, at the
    edge of the yard, on the roof — and the men would fire at it. By some accounts it flipped over
    when hit, then got up or vanished into the dark. In one account a huge clawed hand grabbed a
    man's hair as he stepped outside. In another, a creature shot at in a tree floated to the ground
    instead of falling.
  </p>
  <p>
    It never came inside, and no one was reported hurt. After a lull the household left for town;
    after the police had gone, the creatures were reported back around 3:30 in the morning, this
    time without any shooting, and gone again by daylight. Afterwards, reportedly, the families left
    for Evansville, Indiana. Hundreds of curious people came to look at the farm, and the household
    reportedly tried charging them for admission, information and photographs.
  </p>
  <p>
    That is the whole of its behaviour: one night, a few hours of appearances near a dark house, and
    then nothing, ever. The farmhouse is gone now. What lives on is a town festival — and a phrase.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>There is no creature here to defeat, so the honest assessment is about the explanations.</p>
  <p>
    <strong>The owl explanation fits most of it.</strong> In 2005 the investigator Joe Nickell was
    invited to speak at Hopkinsville's fiftieth-anniversary festival, visited the site, read the
    original clippings and talked with surviving witnesses. In the <em>Skeptical Inquirer</em> the
    next year he matched the descriptions point by point to the great horned owl: about 25 inches
    tall, large yellow eyes, long ear tufts, a big head with no apparent neck, talons, and an
    aggressive streak when defending the nest. His conclusion was "a meteor and a pair of
    territorial owls." The French researcher Renaud Leclet had reached a similar view in 2001.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The light in the sky has an ordinary answer.</strong> Davis and Bloecher themselves
    noted area reports of meteors that evening, and Nickell took the "saucer" to be a bright
    fireball. Brian Dunning of <em>Skeptoid</em> points to the Kappa Cygnid meteor shower active at
    the time.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The other theories are weaker.</strong> Escaped monkeys were proposed early on, but no
    escape was ever shown. Drink has been blamed for decades; one state trooper later said he
    suspected it, yet admitted he saw no sign of it, and the 1955 paper reported officials agreeing
    there was none. A hoax remains possible, but nothing physical supports it either.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The official record is muddled.</strong> Many books say Air Force investigators came the
    next day. Dunning found no corroboration, and later writers note that Project Blue Book's files
    describe the case as never officially reported to the Air Force — though older accounts claim it
    was listed as unidentified, and a set of Blue Book lecture notes reportedly filed it among
    hoaxes. Accounts differ, and none of them is a full investigation.
  </p>
</Section>

    <p>
      Around seven on the evening of Sunday, 21 August 1955, Billy Ray Taylor, a visitor from
      Pennsylvania, went to the well behind the farmhouse at Kelly and came back describing a bright
      light that had crossed the sky. The household paid him little attention. About an hour later a
      barking dog drew Taylor and "Lucky" Sutton outside; they saw a creature, fetched a .22 and a
      shotgun, and began what they later described as hours of shooting at faces in the windows and
      figures on the roof and in the trees.
    </p>
    <p>
      At around 11 p.m. the group reached Hopkinsville police headquarters; the next day's
      <em>Kentucky New Era</em> quoted one of the men as saying simply, "We need help." Police Chief
      Russell Greenwell led more than a dozen officers to the farm, joined by military police from
      Fort Campbell. They searched until after 2 a.m. and found a hole in a screen and nothing else.
      After they left, the creatures were reported back at about 3:30 a.m. By daylight the visitors
      were gone, and the story was on its way to the national papers.

      Center for UFO Studies published in 1978. She noted that the man who first saw the light was
      the one witness who raised immediate doubts, while the others aroused no such suspicion. Half
      a century later Joe Nickell, invited to the 2005 anniversary festival, argued for a fireball
      and a pair of great horned owls defending their nest; Brian Dunning added that the birds would
      have been feeding young in August. Blake Smith has since tested how shot passes through a
      window screen and compared the "flipping" creatures to the way birds tumble.

      reporting in 2021 said the festival had not run since 2019. For the seventieth anniversary in
      2025, the City of Hopkinsville marked the date with an Alien Invasion Day.

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Hopkinsville Goblins are one of the great set pieces of UFO history: a farmhouse under
      siege, a family firing into the dark, a convoy of police cars on a country road. Strip away
      seventy years of retelling and what is left is a frightened household with no telephone, a
      bright light in the sky, and a few hours of shapes they could not identify.
    </p>
    <p>
      Nothing suggests those people were lying; the police chief who met them that night said they
      were genuinely frightened. But the creatures they described fit a large, angry bird far better
      than a crew from another world — and the only real danger at Kelly that night came from the
      shotgun, not the roof.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Light the yard. Count what you see at once. And put the gun down until
      you know what you are aiming at.
    </p>
  </Outro>
