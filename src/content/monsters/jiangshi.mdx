<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is well after dark on a narrow mountain road in western Hunan. You hear a handbell first —
    slow, deliberate, coming closer — and then a voice somewhere ahead calling for the living to
    stand aside and avert their eyes. Local custom is absolute on this point: you do not watch what
    is passing, and you do not get in its way.
  </p>
  <p>
    If you looked anyway, the old accounts say you would see a short procession moving through the
    cold: a Taoist master, an apprentice, and between them a line of the dead — upright, capped,
    robed, seeming to hop forward in unison down the track toward home. For centuries the people of
    these hills believed a corpse could be made to walk back to the village where it was born.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the seventeenth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject: the Jiangshi —
    the "stiff corpse," the hopping dead of Chinese folklore. It is one of the entries where the
    history behind the monster is stranger, and sadder, than the monster itself.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The Jiangshi is woven from several threads of Chinese belief, but the most grounded of them is a
    real occupation. In the Qing era, men from the mountainous west of Hunan often left home to work
    and died far away. Filial duty — reinforced by ancestor worship and feng shui — demanded that
    they be buried in their ancestral ground, and so a trade grew up around carrying the dead home,
    sometimes over a thousand <em>li</em>.
  </p>
  <p>
    The work was done at night, when the roads were thought fit for the dead, and announced by bells
    and gongs so the living could clear the way. A Daoist master and his apprentice led the
    procession, placing a paper talisman on each corpse's forehead and testing the body with a
    peachwood sword. Viewed from a distance, bodies tied upright to flexing bamboo poles appeared to
    rise and fall together — to <em>hop</em>.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The horror of the Jiangshi did not begin as a horror. It began as devotion — the refusal to
    leave a family member buried among strangers, and the belief that a lost soul far from home
    could never truly rest.
  </div>
  <p>
    From that solemn practice, rumour did the rest. Corpse-driving using manual labour gave rise to
    legends of Taoist sorcery that reanimated the dead and marched them home, and some accounts
    suggest the stories were deliberately encouraged — to keep the curious at a respectful distance,
    and, by one theory, to help smugglers scare off anyone who came too close.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The Jiangshi also draws on older strands of belief — that a soul which fails to leave the body
      after an improper or untimely death may animate the corpse, and that an unburied body left too
      long could absorb <em>yang</em> energy and rise. The folklore is a layering of funerary taboo,
      ritual, and genuine anxiety about the dead who have not been laid to rest.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    The Jiangshi of legend is a corpse gone rigid — stiff-limbed, which is precisely why it is said
    to hop rather than walk, arms held out straight ahead for want of flexing joints. Its skin is
    described as greenish-white or mottled, sometimes furred with the mould of the grave, and its
    gaze is vacant. It is blind, the stories insist, and finds the living only by sensing their
    breath.
  </p>
  <p>
    The image most of the world carries, though, comes from the screen: the Qing-dynasty mandarin's
    coat-like robe and tall round-brimmed cap, with a yellow paper talisman hanging from the
    forehead. That costume is a twentieth-century cinematic convention more than a folk constant —
    and once you have seen it, every Jiangshi you imagine will wear it.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>

      Separate the record into its two honest halves. One half is documented cultural history — a
      funerary trade, a set of Taoist rites, and a genre of films. The other half is the
      supernatural creature itself, which lives in folklore and on screen and has never once walked
      into the daylight of the verifiable. Keep the line between them clear.
    </p>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    In folklore the Jiangshi does not stalk by sight. It is blind, and it hunts by the breath of the
    living — which is why the oldest piece of survival advice attached to it is simply to stop
    breathing when it draws near. It feeds not on blood but on <em>qi</em>, the life-force, draining
    it from the living to grow stronger; the more it takes, the more capable it becomes.
  </p>
  <p>
    It moves by hopping, stiff and relentless, though older accounts grant the truly ancient ones
    the ability to run, and even to fly once they have absorbed enough <em>yang</em> energy. It is
    not clever the way a Wendigo is clever. It is persistent — a dead thing pointed in a direction,
    unable to reason with and difficult to turn aside.
  </p>
  <p>
    Against it, tradition offers a whole household of defences: the <em>fulu</em> talisman pressed
    to the forehead to still it, mirrors it cannot bear to face, peachwood to deter it, glutinous
    rice and adzuki beans, and a raised doorsill its rigid legs cannot step across. These are the
    tools of the priest and the frightened family, not the hero.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    The honest assessment here is a matter of categories. The Jiangshi as a reanimated, qi-draining
    corpse has no case file — no body, no recovered specimen, no documented encounter. What is real
    is everything that grew up <em>around</em> the belief.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The trade was real.</strong> The transport of the dead home for ancestral burial in
    western Hunan is documented cultural history, filial duty made into a paid occupation, done at
    night and warned of by bells.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The illusion is explicable.</strong> Corpses carried upright on flexing bamboo poles,
    seen at a distance in the dark, really would appear to bob and hop in unison — a plausible seed
    for the image of the walking dead.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The costume is cinema.</strong> The capped, robed, talisman-browed Jiangshi that the
    world pictures is largely the invention of 1980s Hong Kong filmmakers, not a timeless folk
    portrait. The monster you fear may be wearing a movie's wardrobe.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two cases are not hauntings. They are documented cultural history — the funerary practice
    that seeded the legend, and the film that carried it around the world.
  </p>

    <p>
      In the mountainous west of Hunan, men who died far from home posed a problem of duty. Ancestor
      worship and filial piety held that the dead belonged in their native ground, and that a soul
      buried among strangers could be lost. Families who could not carry a body home themselves, or
      afford easier transport across the hard terrain, turned to those who could — and a trade in
      moving the dead grew up, carrying corpses back across great distances for burial.
    </p>
    <p>
      The work was done after dark and announced with bells and gongs, warning the living to stand
      aside and look away, since to set eyes on the passing dead was thought to invite misfortune. A
      Daoist master and his apprentice led each procession, checking the deceased, testing the body
      with a peachwood sword, and placing a talisman on each forehead. Bodies tied upright to
      flexing bamboo poles, glimpsed at night from a distance, seemed to hop forward in step.
    </p>
    <p>
      Historians treat this corpse-driving as one strand of the Jiangshi legend rather than its
      whole origin, but the connection is direct: a real, solemn occupation, wrapped in ritual and
      rumour, is the most grounded source we have for the image of the dead who walk themselves
      home. The practice faded over the twentieth century as the fears around it grew and the old
      ways were suppressed.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      On 7 November 1985, Golden Harvest released <em>Mr. Vampire</em>, directed by Ricky Lau and
      produced by Sammo Hung. A horror-comedy built on the hopping dead, it became the breakthrough
      success of the Jiangshi genre and established many of the tropes the world now takes for the
      folklore itself — among them the detail, immortalised in its Taiwanese title "Hold Your Breath
      for a Moment," that you survive a Jiangshi by not breathing.
    </p>
    <p>
      Lam Ching-ying's Taoist master, Kau, set the template for the unibrowed priest who battles the
      capped, Qing-robed dead with paper talismans, glutinous rice, mirrors, and peachwood. The film
      spawned a franchise and a wave of imitators across the 1980s, and its hopping corpses
      travelled far beyond Hong Kong — into Taiwanese and Japanese productions, and into the
      imaginations of overseas audiences meeting the creature for the first time.
    </p>
    <p>
      That reach is the point of the case. For a great many people worldwide, the Jiangshi
      <em>is</em> the one from this film — breath held, talisman fluttering, arms outstretched. It
      is a striking example of how a piece of regional folklore, filtered through a single popular
      work, becomes the version everyone remembers.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Jiangshi is not, in the end, a story about a monster. It is a story about the dead who are
      far from home, and the lengths the living once went to bring them back — carrying bodies
      through the mountains by night, ringing a bell, asking strangers only to look away.
    </p>
    <p>
      From that devotion came the rumour, and from the rumour came the hopping corpse, and from the
      corpse came the films that fixed its capped, talisman-browed face in the world's memory. Each
      layer is real in its own way; only the last one hops.
    </p>
    <p>
      So if you are ever on a dark road and you hear a bell coming closer — step aside, lower your
      eyes, and let the dead pass on toward home.
    </p>
    <p>End of journal entry. Mind the threshold. Stay sceptical. And hold your breath.</p>
  </Outro>
