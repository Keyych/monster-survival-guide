<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is the spring of 1979, and you are ten years old, walking home from cram school after dark.
    The shops are shutting. The streetlamps are on. Today, at your desk, a boy from another school
    told you about the woman — the one who waits on corners in a long coat with a mask over her
    mouth, and who stops children to ask a single question.
  </p>
  <p>
    <em>Am I pretty?</em> Say no, and that is the end of you. Say yes, and she lowers the mask, and
    the face underneath is not the face you were promised, and she asks again. You have heard there
    are ways out. Say a certain word three times. Carry a certain sweet. Nobody agrees which. At the
    next corner there is a woman standing under the light, and she is wearing a mask.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirty-second journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is
    Kuchisake-onna, the slit-mouthed woman — less a creature than a rumour, and one of the best
    documented rumour panics of the twentieth century. She never harmed a child. The story about her
    reached almost every child in Japan.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The modern legend has a fairly precise birthplace. Accounts trace it to rural Gifu Prefecture
    late in 1978, where an elderly farm woman reportedly saw a woman with a mouth split towards her
    ears standing near her home and was badly frightened. Sources differ on the town — Yaotsu in
    some versions, a village in Motosu district in others — but they agree on the shape of what
    followed. Local children picked the story up, retold it, and improved it.
  </p>
  <p>
    By January 1979 regional newspapers in the Gifu and Nagoya area had noticed the rumour among
    schoolchildren; accounts differ on which paper ran it first. National weekly magazines took it
    up from March. Folklorist Iikura Yoshiyuki of Kokugakuin University, interviewed by Nippon.com,
    credits the cram schools: they gathered children from different districts in one room each
    evening, and the children carried each other's stories home. Within about six months the
    slit-mouthed woman was known from Aomori in the north to Kagoshima in the south.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Iikura has said she "must be the first purely Japanese urban legend". Not a ghost from a temple
    scroll, not a mountain spirit — a story built by children, in cram schools and on telephones,
    that every adult in the country heard about by summer.
  </div>
  <p>
    Older roots are claimed, and should be handled carefully. Some writers point to Edo-period
    collections of strange tales, among them <em>Kaidan Oi no Tsue</em> and the illustrated{' '}
    <em>Ehon Sayo Shigure</em>, which include a woman who turns to reveal a mouth slit to the ears —
    in one telling, a fox in disguise. These are genuine echoes, but a direct line from them to the
    1979 rumour has not been established. Most of what the children told each other was new.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The rumour also supplied its own backstories, none of them sourced: a cosmetic operation gone
      wrong, three unlucky sisters, a patient who had left a hospital, a grudge centuries old. Their
      sheer number is the tell. A real figure has one history. A rumour grows a new one every time
      someone asks "but why?"
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    The children's description is consistent at its core. She is a tall woman, often said to be
    beautiful, with long hair and a surgical-style mask over the lower half of her face. Many
    versions dress her in red — a coat, a beret, high heels. Beneath the mask, her mouth is slit
    wide towards the ears. In some tellings she carries scissors, a knife or a sickle.
  </p>
  <p>
    Everything else varies, and the variation grew over time. She could run a hundred metres in six
    seconds; in other schools, three. She hated hair pomade, or the smell of it. She loved a kind of
    amber hard candy called <em>bekkō-ame</em> — or detested it, depending on who was telling you.
    Researcher Yoshida Yuki, in a book excerpt published by Bunshun, notes that before the 1979 boom
    she mostly showed her face and gave chase, and that the version in which she attacks became
    standard only as the rumour peaked.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      The one identifying feature of Kuchisake-onna — a woman in a face mask — is also one of the
      most ordinary sights in Japan. The legend works by attaching dread to something you will see a
      dozen times on any walk home. If the description fits half the people on the street, it is not
      a description. It is a fear looking for somewhere to land.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The legend has a script, and the script is the whole monster. She appears at dusk on the route
    home, alone, at a corner. She asks her question. Every answer is a trap: no is fatal, yes leads
    to the unmasking and a second question. The listener's only hope is a trick — an evasive answer
    such as "average", a word chanted three times, a sweet thrown to distract her.
  </p>
  <p>
    That structure is why it spread. A story with a question and a set of escape rules is a story
    children can play with, test on each other, and trade. Each new school added a rule; each new
    rule gave the story another reason to be retold.
  </p>
  <p>
    The adult world responded as if the threat were real. By several accounts, patrols on school
    routes were stepped up in the spring, some schools sent pupils home in groups, and police cars
    were sent out in at least two cities. In June 1979, in Himeji, a woman who had dressed as the
    figure was arrested carrying a knife — the clearest case of the rumour producing a genuine
    hazard. Then, around the start of the summer holidays, it simply stopped. With the children out
    of school and out of cram school, the network that carried it went quiet.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    Kuchisake-onna has no body, so the assessment is about the rumour — and the rumour's weaknesses
    are well recorded.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>She never leaves a witness.</strong> Every account is second-hand at best: a cousin, a
    classmate, a child at another school. Even the founding Gifu sighting is a report of a report,
    and sources cannot agree on the village.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>She depends on a network.</strong> The legend travelled through cram schools, telephone
    calls and weekly magazines, and it faded the moment the holidays broke that network up. A real
    threat does not take August off.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Her real dangers are human.</strong> The best-recorded arrest of the 1979 wave was of a
    woman imitating her. The fear also had real causes: Iikura suggests the figure reflected
    children's unease about the strangers they met on late-night streets after cram school. That
    fear is reasonable. It deserves a reasonable answer, not pomade.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of this makes the children foolish. They were doing what children everywhere do with a good
    story. The panic belonged to the adults who printed it.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    Neither of these is a sighting. The first is the 1979 wave itself, as it was set down in the
    press and recalled by folklorists. The second is what happened when the story crossed the sea.
  </p>

    <p>
      Late in 1978, by the most widely repeated account, an elderly woman in rural Gifu saw a woman
      with a slit mouth near her home. Children took the story and ran with it. Newspapers in the
      region mentioned the rumour in January 1979; weekly magazines carried it nationally from
      March; by June it had reached the far ends of the country. Along the way she acquired the
      mask, the red coat, the question, the blade, the impossible running speed, and a growing list
      of ways to escape her.
    </p>
    <p>
      Adults took it seriously enough to act. Accounts describe patrols on school routes, pupils
      sent home in groups, and police cars dispatched in some towns. In Himeji in June, a woman
      dressed as the figure was arrested carrying a knife. Then the summer holidays arrived, the
      cram schools emptied, and the wave subsided. What remained was a character — one that film and
      television have borrowed ever since.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      In South Korea the slit-mouthed woman became the Red Mask — <em>ppalgan masukeu</em> — a woman
      in a red mask who appears on dim street corners and asks passers-by whether she is pretty.
      Sources disagree on when she arrived. The Korea JoongAng Daily describes a nationwide scare in
      the 1990s, with children hurrying home before dark; Nippon.com dates the legend's overseas
      spread to around 2000; and an informant in the USC Digital Folklore Archives remembers
      classmates trading the story around 2004, when he was seven.
    </p>
    <p>
      The Korean versions changed to suit their tellers. The JoongAng Daily gives her a cosmetic
      operation gone wrong as a backstory; Nippon.com notes variants in which she cannot turn
      corners or climb stairs. The JoongAng Daily also suggests parents found the story useful for
      getting children home early. It is the same pattern seen in Japan: a real worry about streets
      at night, given a face and a question.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      Kuchisake-onna is the rare monster in this guide whose spread can be followed almost month by
      month: a farmhouse in Gifu, a newspaper column, the magazines, the cram schools, the whole
      country, and then silence when the summer holidays began. She is one of the clearest pictures
      we have of how a story becomes a panic, and how a panic ends.
    </p>
    <p>
      The child on the corner in 1979 was right to be wary. Dark streets and strangers are worth
      some caution. But the woman under the streetlight was almost certainly just wearing a mask,
      and the one who played the part in Himeji was arrested for it.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Walk home in company. Ask who actually saw her. And stay sceptical — a
      question with no right answer is a sign you are inside a story.
    </p>
  </Outro>
