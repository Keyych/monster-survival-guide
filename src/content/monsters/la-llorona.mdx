<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is late, and you are walking home along the edge of a river. Perhaps it is a canal in the
    south of Mexico City, perhaps an irrigation ditch in northern New Mexico, perhaps a muddy bend
    of the Rio Grande. The water is louder than it is in daylight. There are no lights on this
    stretch, and you are walking faster than you mean to.
  </p>
  <p>
    Then you hear it, somewhere ahead or behind — you cannot tell which. A woman, crying. Not
    quietly. A long, rising wail that breaks into words, and the words are always the same:{' '}
    <em>¡Ay, mis hijos!</em> Oh, my children. You have heard about her since you were small. You
    know you are not supposed to go and look.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the twenty-ninth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is La Llorona,
    the weeping woman — perhaps the best-known legend in Mexico and one of the most widely told in
    the American Southwest. She has no body to examine and no confirmed sighting, but she has
    something more durable than either: a story that millions of people were raised on.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The tale has many versions but one core. A woman, abandoned by the man she loved, drowns her
    children in a river. Grief or punishment condemns her to search the riverbanks for them forever,
    weeping. In some tellings she drowned them in rage; in others she simply lost them to the water.
    Folklorists Norma Elia Cantú and Kathleen Alcalá describe this core as the shared spine of the
    variants told throughout what the scholar Américo Paredes called "Greater Mexico".
  </p>
  <p>
    Where that story began is genuinely disputed, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling
    something. The case for deep Indigenous roots starts in the 16th century, with the Franciscan
    friar Bernardino de Sahagún and the Nahua scholars who worked with him on the great
    encyclopaedia now called the Florentine Codex. Its twelfth book lists omens said to have
    troubled Tenochtitlan before the Spanish arrived. The sixth is a woman heard crying out at night
    for her children, asking where she could take them. Another book of the same work describes the
    goddess Cihuacoatl, who, in the codex's words, "walked about weeping at night".
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The omen is real, and old. What is not established is the line connecting it to the woman by the
    river. That line was drawn later, by people looking backwards — and every generation since has
    drawn it a little differently.
  </div>
  <p>
    The caution matters. Sahagún gathered these accounts decades after the conquest of 1521,
    probably in the 1550s, and the codex itself dates to the 1570s. Historian Amy Fuller notes that
    the codices went unpublished until the 19th century, and that the legend as we know it does not
    appear in print until the late 19th or early 20th century. Stephen Winick, writing on the
    Library of Congress's Folklife Today blog, found the earliest literary treatment he could locate
    in an 1849 poem by Manuel Carpio — and, intriguingly, an 1866 Spanish tale of a weeping ghost
    called La Llorona, set in the Andalusian town of Rota.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      Scholars have split on this for decades. Paredes argued the literary origin story was grafted
      onto an older Indigenous cycle of supernatural women; Bacil Kirtley pointed to European
      parallels, from German "White Lady" ghosts to Medea. Winick's own reading is that Spanish
      elements reached Mexico but the roots remain largely Indigenous. A strong modern tradition
      also equates La Llorona with La Malinche, the Nahua woman who interpreted for Hernán Cortés.
      The most honest summary is that La Llorona is a Mexican story with several ancestors, and
      nobody has a full family tree.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    The descriptions agree on very little except the sound. She is a woman, usually in white,
    usually near water, usually at night. Some tellings give her long black hair, a veil, or no
    visible face at all; the Smithsonian Latino Center records a Mexico City version in which she
    drifts through the neighbourhoods as fog rather than walking. The crying is the constant. She is
    heard long before she is seen, and many who "encounter" her never see anything at all.
  </p>
  <p>
    What she wants also shifts. In most versions she is searching for her drowned children and may
    take any child she finds near the water in their place. In others she lures men out at night, or
    simply passes by in grief. The older figure in the Florentine Codex is different again: a voice
    of foreboding, lamenting for her children because something terrible is coming. Stephanie Wood,
    writing on that codex image, observes that it is less frightening than later depictions.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      Sound behaves strangely over water and in canyons at night. Wind, animals, birds, and distant
      people can all produce cries that seem to come from everywhere and nowhere. If you hear
      weeping by a river, the useful question is not who she is — it is whether someone living needs
      help, and whether you are standing somewhere safe.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    La Llorona does not operate in the woods. She operates at the edge of the bed, and at the edge
    of the water. The legend is passed from adult to child, usually as a warning: do not play by the
    river, do not go out after dark, do not wander off alone. Writing for New Mexico Magazine in
    2025, Jennifer Levin put the regional version plainly — "New Mexico children don't play in
    arroyos for fear of La Llorona."
  </p>
  <p>
    The setting adapts to the landscape. In northern New Mexico she haunts arroyos, acequias, and
    the Santa Fe River. Along the Rio Grande, a 2007 local history by Paul Harden described a
    version centred on the river from the Socorro bosque south to Juárez, with a variant set in
    Mesilla. In Texas the story became a place-name, Woman Hollering Creek near Seguin, which Sandra
    Cisneros later used for her fiction. Cantú recalls hearing of La Llorona haunting the Platte
    River in Lincoln, Nebraska — carried north by families who moved for work.
  </p>
  <p>
    And she is staged. Each autumn around the Day of the Dead, a production of La Llorona is
    performed on the canals of Xochimilco at the Embarcadero de Cuemanco, with audiences watching
    from torch-lit trajineras. Its producers present it as the "original" story; Fuller, reviewing
    the codex evidence, does not think the record supports that claim. Both things can be true at
    once: a modern performance, and a genuinely living tradition.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    There is no creature here to weaken. La Llorona has never left a footprint, a photograph, or a
    victim that any credible source attributes to her. The honest assessment is of the story.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Her strongest claim to age is also her weakest.</strong> The sixth omen and Cihuacoatl's
    nightly weeping are genuine 16th-century records, but they were written down after the conquest,
    by people who already knew how it ended. Omens recorded in hindsight tell us how a defeat was
    remembered, not what was heard in 1509.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The famous plot is younger than it sounds.</strong> The abandoned mother and the drowned
    children do not appear in print until the 19th century, and a weeping ghost of the same name
    turns up in a Spanish tale of 1866. That does not make La Llorona a European import; it makes
    her a story that has absorbed many sources and kept the ones that rang true.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Her real power is what she is used for.</strong> Writers from Octavio Paz to Chicana
    authors of the borderlands have read her as a figure of loss, conquest, and the women blamed for
    both. A legend that can carry that much meaning is not going to be disproved. It can only be
    understood.
  </p>
</Section>

    Neither of these is a sighting. The first is the oldest written trace of a weeping woman in the
    Valley of Mexico; the second is the legend as it is still told along the rivers and ditches of
    the American Southwest.

    <p>
      In the twelfth book of the Florentine Codex, the Nahua account of the conquest opens with a
      series of omens said to have appeared in the years before the Spanish arrived. The sixth is a
      woman heard weeping and shouting in the night. In one English rendering she cries, "Oh, my
      children, we are about to go," and at other times asks where she can take them. Winick dates
      the omen to around 1509, roughly a decade before Cortés landed.
    </p>
    <p>
      The codex illustrates her: a woman with her hands clasped to her chest and tears on her cheek,
      drawn in a European-influenced style. Scholars have suggested she may be the earliest image of
      La Llorona, and the codex elsewhere links nightly weeping to Cihuacoatl. But the material was
      collected decades after 1521 and lay unpublished for centuries. It shows that a weeping woman
      belonged to how the conquest was remembered. It does not show that the riverbank ghost already
      existed.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      North of the border, La Llorona is less an omen than a household rule. In northern New Mexico
      she is said to search the waterways for her drowned children, and children are warned away
      from arroyos and acequias because she might mistake them for her own. Storyteller Ray John de
      Aragón, author of <em>The Legend of La Llorona</em>, is among those who have kept the regional
      versions in print. Along the Rio Grande, Harden recorded a version in which a woman from
      Juárez drowns her children in the river near Mesilla, and noted its plain purpose: keeping
      children away from the river at night.
    </p>
    <p>
      Cantú and Alcalá, introducing their 2024 collection <em>Weeping Women</em>, trace the story on
      both banks of the Rio Grande — a river that became a border in 1848 — and beyond it, into
      Texas, the Midwest, and Latina and Chicana writing. The Smithsonian Latino Center notes that
      in the United States she adapts to her new country and may weep in Spanish, English, or
      Spanglish. No one in these sources claims proof. What they document is a story doing its work,
      generation after generation.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      La Llorona is the oldest voice in this guide and one of the hardest to pin down. A weeping
      woman appears in an account of the conquest written down by Nahua scholars and a Spanish
      friar; a weeping ghost of the same name turns up in an Andalusian tale; and somewhere between
      them, over centuries, Mexican families built a story that has outlived every attempt to fix
      its origin.
    </p>
    <p>
      What the legend has always said, underneath the grief, is simple. The river is dangerous at
      night. Children should not be near it alone. Those who told you about her were not trying to
      frighten you for its own sake. They were trying to bring you home.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Keep away from the water after dark. Listen before you look. And when
      you hear the story, remember who is telling it, and why.
    </p>
  </Outro>
