<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is about two o'clock in the morning at the end of June 1988, and you are seventeen, driving
    home from a night shift at McDonald's in a 1976 Toyota Celica. The road runs out of Bishopville,
    South Carolina, through flat farm country and over the bridge at Scape Ore Swamp, with nothing
    lit for miles. About a mile past the bridge, a tyre goes.
  </p>
  <p>
    You get out and start on the wheel by whatever light you have. When you look up, something is
    coming out of the trees towards you on two legs. It is tall — taller than a man — and wet, and
    green, and its eyes are red. You get back in the car. You feel it on the door handle, then on
    the roof, and you swerve until it falls away. At home, by your account, there are scratches on
    the roof and a mirror is damaged.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirty-ninth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the Lizard
    Man of Scape Ore Swamp — a creature that existed, as far as the record shows, as one teenager's
    story, a handful of damaged cars, and a summer in which a small town discovered that a monster
    can be very good for business.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The Lizard Man has no folklore behind it. There is no older regional tradition of a reptile man
    in Lee County, no warning handed down through families. It begins in the summer of 1988 with a
    sheriff's office, a teenager named Christopher Davis, and a couple whose car had been damaged in
    the night.
  </p>
  <p>
    Davis's encounter, by later reconstructions, took place around 2am on 29 June 1988 on Browntown
    Road, near the swamp that lies a few miles south of Bishopville. He kept it to himself at first,
    fearing nobody would believe him. In mid-July, Tom and Mary Waye reported that their Ford LTD
    had been chewed and scratched overnight. Lee County Sheriff Liston Truesdale connected the two,
    and on Monday 18 July he mentioned a strange creature to Jan Easterling, a reporter checking in
    with local police. Her story — a little over 200 words — ran on the front page of{' '}
    <em>The State</em> the next day, headlined as a "Lizard Man" lurking in a Lee County swamp.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    This creature was named by a newspaper headline within a day of becoming public. Everything that
    followed — the crowds, the reward, the T-shirts, the hoaxers — came after the name, not before
    it. Keep that order of events in mind.
  </div>
  <p>
    What happened next is the real story. By the Associated Press's account two days later, the
    swamp was crowded with television crews and onlookers. A Columbia radio station, WCOS, offered a
    $1 million reward for the creature's capture. According to the{' '}
    <em>South Carolina Encyclopedia</em>, citing <em>Time</em>, the state was taking more than 200
    calls a day, some from as far away as New Zealand and Germany, and the governor, Carroll
    Campbell, was fielding questions about it.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      Bishopville did not resist. Lizard Man T-shirts and hats sold quickly, shops did a trade in
      green "lizard juice" lemonade, and Davis signed autographs at a "Lizard Man Information
      Center" in a local restaurant; he later estimated he made about $3,000 from his story. Decades
      on, the South Carolina Cotton Museum in Bishopville keeps a Lizard Man exhibit, including
      footprint casts from 1988, and since 2018 the town has held festivals in the creature's name.
      The monster is uncertain. Its economic value has been well documented.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    The core description comes from Davis, and it is short. He described the creature as "green,
    wet-like, about seven feet tall and had three fingers," with red eyes — later retellings add
    scaly skin. That is essentially the whole portrait: an upright, man-sized reptile,
    three-fingered, red-eyed, coming out of the water.
  </p>
  <p>
    Beyond Davis, the description frays quickly. Of the dozen or so strange occurrences enthusiasts
    have since tied to the creature, the <em>Post and Courier</em> notes that few described anything
    lizard-like at all; many witnesses spoke of something brown and hairy. The physical evidence
    from 1988 — tracks near the Waye home, and short hairs caught in the trim of their car — was
    examined by a state wildlife biologist, Matt Knox, who said the hairs came from a red fox and
    that the tracks might be a bear's.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      The only fully lizard-like account in the record is the first one, and it was given by a
      frightened teenager, at night, by the light of a broken-down car. Every later "Lizard Man" was
      reported after the name and the drawings were already on T-shirts. Before you describe what
      you saw, ask whether you are describing it — or the shirt.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    If the creature has a habit, it is cars. In Davis's account it grabbed the door and landed on
    the roof. The Wayes' car was said to have been chewed. In 2008 a Bishopville couple found their
    van's fender and grille bitten, and in 2015 a comedian on national television joked that it had
    damaged his rental car. The Lizard Man is, in practice, a story told about damaged vehicles near
    a swamp.
  </p>
  <p>
    The other pattern is timing. The reports cluster tightly in the summer of 1988 and then thin
    out, returning in brief bursts — 2008, a few mentions around 2011, a grainy photograph in 2015 —
    each time with a news cycle attached. Between bursts, there is nothing: no body, no road kill,
    no clear picture.
  </p>
  <p>
    It also attracts imitators. In August 1988 an airman from nearby Shaw Air Force Base, Kenneth
    Orr, told Sheriff Truesdale he had shot and wounded the creature on his way to the base, and
    produced a sketch along with blood and scales from his car. The sketch looked like the T-shirt
    art on sale in town and the scales appeared to be from a fish. Orr admitted the hoax, saying he
    did it to keep the legend going, and was charged.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>There is no creature here to fight, so the honest assessment is of the evidence.</p>
  <p>
    <strong>The physical traces pointed elsewhere.</strong> The hairs from the Waye car were
    identified as red fox; the tracks were possibly bear. In 2008 the blood on the Rawsons' van was,
    by later reporting, identified by a forensic lab as coming from a dog, and local authorities had
    suggested a coyote. Each time an object could be tested, it came back as an ordinary animal.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The founding account cannot be re-examined.</strong> It rests on one witness, alone, at
    night, who told no one for weeks. The damage to his car survives only as description, and his
    account was retold many times that summer, for reporters and for autographs. Davis died in 2009,
    and Truesdale in 2015, so the two people closest to the beginning can no longer be asked.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The explanations were offered at the time.</strong> In the same week the story broke,
    biologist Matt Knox suggested — with evident scepticism — that Davis might have seen a drunk man
    getting up out of a muddy ditch. Locals have since repeated a rumour that a butterbean farmer
    used to dress up to scare off thieves; his own family say they do not know whether it is true.
    Neither is proven. Both require less than a seven-foot reptile.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Everyone had a reason to keep it alive.</strong> A confessed hoaxer, a radio station
    with a reward, shops with shirts to sell, and a retired deputy who has admitted playing animal
    calls over his patrol car's speaker to scare tourists. That does not make Davis a liar. It does
    mean the later record is badly contaminated.
  </p>
</Section>

    <p>
      Around 2am on 29 June 1988, seventeen-year-old Christopher Davis was driving home from a night
      shift when his car blew a tyre on Browntown Road, about a mile past the Scape Ore bridge. As
      he changed it, he said, a seven-foot, green, three-fingered creature with red eyes came at him
      from the swamp, grabbed the door handle as he got in, and jumped on to the roof until he
      swerved it off. At first he kept it to himself.
    </p>
    <p>
      In mid-July, Tom and Mary Waye reported that their car had been chewed up overnight. Sheriff
      Liston Truesdale investigated, found tracks near the Waye home that were not from a cow or a
      deer, and told the Associated Press he was hearing from callers he considered "reputable
      people." Davis agreed to be hypnotised to recall more. A state wildlife biologist identified
      hairs from the car as red fox and suggested the tracks might be a bear's. In August, airman
      Kenneth Orr's claim to have shot the creature collapsed into a confessed hoax.
    </p>

    <p>
      Early on 28 February 2008, by the account posted to a Free Republic forum thread from{' '}
      <em>The State</em>, Bob and Dixie Rawson of Bishopville found their van with a chewed front
      fender, bite marks through the grille and blood on its front and sides, and their cats gone.
      The local news ran it, and the Lizard Man was back. Then-Lee County Sheriff E.J. Melvin told
      CNN, "you look at the damage to the vehicle, it's hard to say." Tom Biscardi of the California
      group Searching for Bigfoot came to make moulds and take samples; local authorities had
      suggested a coyote. In May a forensic lab matched the blood to a dog, a finding Biscardi and
      Dixie Rawson both disputed.
    </p>
    <p>
      In August 2015 a woman said she had photographed the creature at the edge of the woods as she
      left a church service near Bishopville, and a hunter sent a local station video he said he
      shot at Scape Ore Swamp that May. The images were grainy and the witnesses went unnamed in the
      coverage. The story made national news, and Stephen Colbert joked about it. Nothing further
      was established.

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Lizard Man is one of the most human entries in this guide. A frightened teenager on a dark
      road told a story, a sheriff took it seriously, a newspaper gave it a name, and a small farm
      town found, for one hot summer, that the whole country wanted to visit. Some of what followed
      was sincere. Some of it was a fox, a dog, a coyote. And some of it was invented on purpose and
      later admitted.
    </p>
    <p>
      Whatever Christopher Davis saw at two in the morning on Browntown Road, the creature that
      lives on in Bishopville is the one on the T-shirts — and that one, at least, is entirely real.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Fix the tyre somewhere lit. Photograph the damage. And stay sceptical —
      a monster that sells this well will always find another witness.
    </p>
  </Outro>
