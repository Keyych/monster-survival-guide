<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is late on a winter night in February 1997, and you are awake somewhere in America with the
    radio on. The show is Art Bell's <em>Coast to Coast AM</em> — hours of open lines, strange
    callers and stranger stories, beamed across the country in the small hours. A man comes on the
    line and gives his name as Mel Waters. He lives, he says, near Ellensburg, in central
    Washington, and there is a hole on his land.
  </p>
  <p>
    He describes it calmly. A round opening in the ground, a few strides across, ringed with stones.
    Rubbish has gone into it for years and it has never filled. He once lowered a weighted fishing
    line into it, reel after reel, until he had run out more than eighty thousand feet, and it never
    touched bottom. Then he starts telling you what else the hole can do, and the stories get
    stranger.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirty-sixth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is Mel's Hole
    — not a creature, but a place that may never have existed outside a radio studio. It is an
    unverified call-in claim, and that is how this entry treats it from first page to last.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    Mel's Hole has no folklore behind it. It is not an old warning, a regional tradition or a story
    handed down in the Kittitas Valley. It begins on a precise date: according to the Ellensburg
    <em>Daily Record</em>, the first call to Art Bell's programme came on 21 February 1997, and the
    caller rang back three days later. Further calls reportedly followed in 2000 and twice in 2002,
    and the show rebroadcast the segments over the years, which kept the story alive long after the
    first night.
  </p>
  <p>
    The caller never gave an exact location. He and those who later claimed to have found the hole
    have only ever pointed to Manastash Ridge, roughly nine or ten miles west and south-west of
    Ellensburg. In the follow-up calls the story grew a second act: federal agents, he said, had
    taken control of the land, and he had been paid to lease it and leave — to Australia, by his
    account. Then the calls stopped.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    This place has a name because a voice on the radio gave it one. There is no deed, no photograph
    and no map — only a first name, a hillside, and a story told to a national audience in the
    middle of the night. Keep that order of events in mind; it is the whole shape of this entry.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The story spread the way late-night radio stories did at the end of the 1990s: from the
      broadcast to listeners, and from listeners to the early internet. By 2002 the
      <em>Seattle Times</em> was reporting on a chat site devoted to the hole and an amateur search
      party gathering in Ellensburg. By 2008 the Library of Congress had given it a subject heading
      of its own — catalogued, tellingly, as a "legendary place". The one Indigenous voice often
      quoted in the coverage, a local man known as Red Elk, speaks for himself in those reports and
      not for any community's tradition; the hole is not part of any recorded tribal lore.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    As described on air, the hole was about nine feet across, with smooth walls and a low ring of
    stone around the top. Accounts of the calls give it a growing list of powers: rubbish, and in
    some retellings dead livestock, thrown in without ever filling it; a neighbour's dead dog that
    reappeared alive; radios near the opening picking up old broadcasts; a beam of "black light"
    rising from it. Retellings of the calls also mention a Second World War pistol and a red
    envelope of dimes.
  </p>
  <p>
    Nothing in that list has ever been seen by anyone but the caller. The physical description is
    plain enough to match almost any open shaft in dry country; the extraordinary parts rest
    entirely on his word. Even the details are unsettled — different reports give the depth as
    "thousands of feet" or as more than fifteen miles, and the land as nine miles west of town or
    ten miles south-west.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      If you ever find a hole in these hills, it is not this one. The real openings in this country
      are old mine workings — unlit, unmapped, often on private land and liable to collapse at the
      edge. They do not need to be bottomless to kill you. Keep well back, and keep children and
      dogs back too.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    Mel's Hole operates the way a broadcast does. It appears when someone rings in, it grows with
    each new call, and it goes quiet when the calls stop. The first account was a strange hole on a
    farm. The second added soldiers and a government cover story. By 2000, according to a 2023
    retelling in the <em>Yakima Herald-Republic</em>, the caller was describing a lease worth three
    million dollars a year, a kidnapping off a bus near Olympia, and twelve lost days that ended in
    San Francisco.
  </p>
  <p>
    Around that core, a second layer formed: the searchers. Since the late 1990s, listeners and
    amateur investigators have walked Manastash Ridge with topographic maps, GPS units and metal
    detectors. They have found cattle gates, clear-cuts and old driveways. They have not found the
    hole. Each failed search, rather than closing the case, tends to produce a new theory about why
    it cannot be found — most often that the government has hidden it.
  </p>
  <p>
    That is the pattern worth naming. Nothing physical has ever entered the record. What has grown,
    every few years, is the story — and a story that explains its own lack of evidence is very hard
    to disprove and very easy to keep telling.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>There is no creature here, so the honest assessment is about the claim.</p>
  <p>
    <strong>The witness cannot be found.</strong> Local reporters, according to the
    <em>Daily Record</em>, found no public record that a Mel Waters lived in Kittitas County, owned
    rural property there, or had a wife working at Central Washington University. An anonymous
    caller who has sold the land and left the country is not a source anyone can check.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The geology says no.</strong> In 2002, Pat Pringle of the state Department of Natural
    Resources told the <em>Seattle Times</em> the rock there is brittle, making so deep a hole
    unlikely, and that the Earth's heat would snap a line long before eighty thousand feet. In 2012,
    DNR geologist Jack Powell told the <em>Daily Record</em> that a hole that deep would collapse
    under heat and pressure — the deepest borehole ever drilled, in Russia, reached about forty
    thousand feet.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>A checkable detail fails.</strong> As the <em>Yakima Herald-Republic</em> points out,
    the caller's 1943 Roosevelt dimes cannot exist: Roosevelt did not appear on the dime until 1946,
    and the "B" mint mark he described is not a real one. In later calls he reportedly explained
    this with a parallel world. That explanation is itself the clearest sign of what kind of story
    this is.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>There is a likely real hole.</strong> Powell identified an old gold-mine shaft
    north-west of Ellensburg — fenced, on private land, perhaps a few hundred feet deep when worked
    — and suggested the caller probably knew of it. None of this proves a hoax by any named person.
    It simply means the ordinary explanation needs nothing that the extraordinary one does.
  </p>
</Section>

    <p>
      On 21 February 1997, a man identifying himself as Mel Waters called Art Bell's late-night
      programme and described a hole on his rural property west of Ellensburg: roughly nine feet
      across, impossible to fill, and deep enough to swallow more than eighty thousand feet of
      weighted line. He added a dead dog that came back to life and radios that played programmes
      from the past. Three days later he called again, saying the government had taken an interest
      in the land.
    </p>
    <p>
      He reportedly called again in 2000, and twice in 2002, each time adding to the story — a
      lease, a move abroad, a kidnapping. By 2008 the claim was being picked apart on public forums:
      posters on the Straight Dope message board argued that nylon line would fail from the heat
      long before the claimed depth, and that no one could tell by feel when so much line had
      reached a bottom. Those are forum arguments, not measurements, but they reached the obvious
      problems first.
    </p>

    <p>
      In 2001, according to the <em>Daily Record</em>, a Seattle-area online group studying the hole
      asked DNR geologist Jack Powell about local geology. He gave them a talk, then led them to an
      old gold-mine shaft north-west of Ellensburg and told them it was the likely root of the
      legend. In April 2002 the <em>Seattle Times</em> followed a search party of around thirty out
      of Ellensburg — with maps, metal detectors and a GPS unit — onto the ridge. They found
      nothing, and the group's own sceptic, Tim Withee, called the outing "like adult cartoons."
    </p>
    <p>
      Red Elk, described in the coverage as an intertribal medicine man from the Kittitas Valley,
      told reporters his father had shown him the hole around 1961, and later said the government
      now controlled it. A local amateur researcher interviewed by KVAL in 2012 said he had led
      expeditions but never seen it. By 2012, Powell's conclusion was blunt: the hole on Manastash
      Ridge does not exist. Nothing found since has changed that.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      Mel's Hole is the only entry in this guide that began, as far as anyone can show, as a voice
      on the radio. A man gave a first name, described a hole on a hillside, and told a national
      audience things no one could check. Nearly thirty years later, no one has found the man, the
      deed or the hole — only an old mine shaft behind a fence and a great many people who went
      looking.
    </p>
    <p>
      That is not a mystery so much as a lesson. A story told with confidence, at night, to people
      who want to believe it, can outlive every attempt to find its source. The geology is settled,
      the records are empty, and the details that can be checked do not hold. What remains is a good
      story — and a ridge with real heat, real fences and real shafts.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Check the caller. Stay back from the edge. And stay sceptical — the
      deepest holes are the ones dug in the telling.
    </p>
  </Outro>
