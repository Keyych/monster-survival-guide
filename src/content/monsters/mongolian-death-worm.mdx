<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is July in the Galbyn Gobi, the deep southern desert where the gravel plains run to the
    horizon and the heat comes off the ground in sheets. A herder is walking you past a patch of
    bare, rust-coloured sand and he will not step on it. Something lives under there, he says — a
    thing as thick as a man's arm and the colour of fresh blood, no head and no tail you can tell
    apart, and you do not want to be near it when it rises.
  </p>
  <p>
    He has never seen it. His father never saw it. But everyone in the camp believes in it
    completely, and everyone can describe it in the same minute detail — the way it rears up before
    it strikes, the way a man or a camel simply drops. They call it olgoi-khorkhoi, the
    large-intestine worm, because that is exactly what it is said to look like.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the nineteenth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject: the Mongolian
    Death Worm — one of the most famous cryptids on earth, and one of the least evidenced. This is
    an entry where honesty is the whole point, because after a hundred years of searching, the most
    useful thing we can tell you is how little there is to find.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The worm came to the outside world through a sceptic. In the early 1920s the American
    paleontologist Roy Chapman Andrews led the American Museum of Natural History's Central Asiatic
    expeditions into the Gobi — the trips that would turn up the first recognised dinosaur eggs.
    Mongolian officials pressed him about the allghoi khorkhoi and asked him to catch one. He wrote
    the account down and published it, but he did not believe it.
  </p>
  <p>
    Andrews was blunt about his sources. None of the people describing the creature, he noted, had
    ever actually seen it — they had only heard of it, repeated it, and believed it. He recorded the
    tale as hearsay from a culture that held it deeply, not as a report of an animal he thought was
    real. That distinction has been lost in nearly every retelling since.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The man who introduced the Death Worm to the West did not think it existed. He wrote it down the
    way a careful traveller writes down a local belief — faithfully, and without endorsing a word of
    it.
  </div>
  <p>
    The legend sat quietly until the Czech cryptozoologist Ivan Mackerle revived it, first in his
    1987 book and then in person, leading small groups into the Gobi in 1990 and 1992 to hunt the
    worm directly. He is the reason the creature is famous today. He also never found it — and by
    his final expedition he had concluded it was most likely a myth, or a trick the desert heat
    plays on the mind.
  </p>
</Section>

  <p>
    The description is remarkably consistent, which is part of what makes it compelling. Witnesses
    agree it is a fat, sausage-shaped worm around two to five feet long, dark red, with no
    distinguishable head or tail — hence "large-intestine worm." It is said to live just below the
    surface of the hottest, most barren sand, surfacing mainly in the warmest months.
  </p>
  <p>
    Then come the powers, and the powers are where the trouble starts. The worm is credited with
    killing at a distance — spraying a corrosive, lethal venom, or discharging an electric shock
    strong enough to drop a camel. No known animal kills that way across open ground, and the
    "venom" in many accounts is tied to the yellow goyo plant the worm supposedly lives among. These
    are the hallmarks of a story growing in the retelling, not a biology anyone has observed.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      When a herder points out a red "worm," look closely before you flinch. The leading natural
      explanation is the Tartar sand boa (Eryx tataricus), a blunt, thick-bodied, reddish burrowing
      snake native to the region — in a 1983 test, locals shown a specimen agreed it was their
      olgoi-khorkhoi. Legless lizards and amphisbaenians (worm-lizards) are the other usual
      suspects. You are far likelier to be looking at a shy, harmless reptile than at anything new
      to science.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    On paper, the worm's method is terrifyingly simple — it waits beneath the sand and kills
    whatever comes too close, by venom or by shock, without ever being properly seen. In practice,
    that "without ever being properly seen" is the entire problem. The behaviour is reported, never
    recorded; feared, never filmed.
  </p>
  <p>
    The pattern that actually repeats is not the worm's but the expeditions'. Andrews recorded the
    tale and moved on to his fossils. Mackerle baited the sand, built a motor-driven "thumper" to
    summon the creature by vibration, and even set off small buried charges — and came home with
    nothing. Later searches, including Richard Freeman's 2005 trip and David Farrier's in 2009,
    ended the same way. The one consistent behaviour of the Death Worm is its absence.
  </p>
  <p>
    What is real is the Gobi. It is one of the harshest deserts on the planet, and it kills people
    the ordinary ways — heatstroke, dehydration, cold nights, and the simple fact of being a very
    long way from help. Those are the hazards a herder is genuinely warning you about when he steers
    you off the bare red sand.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    This is the entry where the honest assessment is the whole point, so here it is plainly: there
    is nothing here to fight, because there is nothing here that has ever been caught.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The evidence is absent.</strong> No specimen, no carcass, no photograph, no verified
    attack. A hundred years of interest and multiple dedicated expeditions have produced testimony
    and nothing else.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The powers collapse.</strong> Killing by electric discharge or sprayed venom across open
    sand has no parallel in the animal kingdom, and the venom detail is entangled with a desert
    plant. These are the features most likely to be invention.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The animal has an ordinary explanation.</strong> A blunt, reddish, burrowing snake — the
    Tartar sand boa — fits the core description, and locals have confirmed the match. Add
    worm-lizards and the heat-haze of the open desert, and the legend has everything it needs
    without a new species.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of this makes the belief foolish, or the herders' caution wrong. It means the thing to
    respect is the desert, and the thing to be sceptical of is the worm.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two cases bookend the Death Worm's modern life: the account that carried it out of the
    Gobi, and the searches that went looking and found nothing.
  </p>

    <p>
      In the early 1920s, leading the American Museum of Natural History's Central Asiatic
      expeditions through the Gobi, paleontologist Roy Chapman Andrews was repeatedly questioned by
      Mongolian officials about the allghoi khorkhoi — a blood-red, intestine-shaped worm said to
      kill at a touch. He was asked, in effect, to catch one for the government. He wrote the
      request and the description down and later published them.
    </p>
    <p>
      Crucially, Andrews did not endorse the tale. He observed that none of those describing the
      creature had ever seen it themselves; they had only heard of it and believed in it firmly. He
      treated the account as a faithfully recorded local belief, not as evidence of a real animal —
      a distinction stripped away in most of the sensational retellings that followed, which cite
      his book as if he had vouched for the worm.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      The Czech engineer and cryptozoologist Ivan Mackerle is the reason the Death Worm is a
      household name. After writing about it in 1987, he led small expeditions into the Gobi in 1990
      and 1992, searching the southern desert for weeks at a time. Inspired by the sandworms of{' '}
      <em>Dune</em>, he built a motor-driven "thumper" to lure the creature up by vibration and set
      off small buried charges in the dunes.
    </p>
    <p>
      Nothing surfaced. Mackerle returned a third time in 2004, searching from the air with an
      ultralight, and again found no trace. By the end he had come to doubt the creature himself,
      concluding it was most likely a myth or a product of the desert heat on the mind. Later
      searches by other investigators ended the same way — a great deal of sincere effort, and not a
      single specimen to show for it.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>
