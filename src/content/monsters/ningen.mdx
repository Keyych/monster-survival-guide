<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is night in the Antarctic Sea, and you are on the deck of a research whaling ship, or so the
    story goes. The water is black and flat between the floes. Someone on watch points at a shape
    that has surfaced off the bow — white, smooth, far too big to be a seal, far too still to be a
    whale. For a moment it looks like a body. It has arms. It has something like a face.
  </p>
  <p>
    Then you look again, and you realise you are not on a ship at all. You are reading a forum post.
    It is May 2002, the board is the Occult section of the Japanese site 2channel, and the person
    telling you this has no name, no ship, and no photograph. They are passing on what a friend told
    them. That is the whole of the evidence, and it is where every version of the Ningen begins.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the forty-third journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the Ningen,
    a giant pale humanoid said to haunt Antarctic waters. It is not folklore in the old sense and
    not a reported sighting. It is internet lore, built in public by anonymous posters, and this
    entry will treat it as exactly that.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The most careful account of the Ningen's origins is an academic paper by Felicity Greenland and
    Philip Hayward, published in the island-studies journal <em>Shima</em> in 2020. They trace the
    story to a single post made on 11 May 2002 in a 2channel Occult thread about giant and monster
    fish. The poster, writing anonymously and describing themselves as a part-timer, said an insider
    they called "Mr F" had told them about something recorded during Japan's research whaling in the
    Antarctic.
  </p>
  <p>
    According to the post, the whaling programme logged the whales it did not catch, and alongside
    them were "objects" that no one could explain. Some were said to have four limbs and a head; one
    was said to look like two human upper bodies joined together. The post added that an official
    announcement would damage the credibility of the research whaling itself. A dedicated thread
    followed the same day. The original poster announced a "temporary" break within a week and, by
    the paper's account, never came back. Other posters kept the threads going until late 2003.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The Ningen has a name, a size, and a habitat, but it has never had a witness. Every detail
    arrived second-hand through an anonymous account, and the story has been growing ever since.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The name is a small puzzle of its own. Greenland and Hayward report that Mr F said an
      unofficial document used one term for the things, which he said was being misread, and that
      the right reading was <em>hitogata buttai</em>, "human-shaped objects". Then he added that the
      crews just called them <em>ningen</em>. In ordinary Japanese, <em>ningen</em> means "human
      being". The paper notes it also sounds close to <em>ningyo</em>, the mermaid-like fish of
      Japanese folklore, and that writing it in katakana gives it a faintly scientific, alien feel.{' '}
      <em>Hitogata</em> survived alongside it as an alternative name.
    </p>
  </div>
  <p>
    The thread also borrowed authority from an older tale: a reputed 1958 sighting of an "Antarctic
    Godzilla" by the crew of the icebreaker <em>Sōya-maru</em>. That story is a separate legend.
    Linking the two made the Ningen feel older than it was. It does not make either of them true.
  </p>
</Section>

  <p>
    The 2002 post gives the core of the description: the whole body is white, it comes up from the
    water the way a whale does, and it is, in the paper's translation, "a few tens of metres long."
    Later English-language write-ups settled on twenty to thirty metres, a humanlike shape with arms
    and sometimes legs or five-fingered hands, and a face that shows only eyes and a mouth. Others
    give it a tail or fins, like a mermaid. Most accounts say it is seen at night.
  </p>
  <p>
    Very little of this is stable. The number of limbs changes, the tail comes and goes, and the
    size has drifted with every retelling. That is what you would expect from a description that was
    never anchored to a single sighting. Each new poster, artist, or video maker has added the
    detail that frightened them most.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      A large, white, roughly human shape in polar water at night describes a great many ordinary
      things: a slab of ice, a pale whale, foam over a swell, the underside of a big ray. Before you
      file a pale shape as a Ningen, remember the Southern Ocean is full of ice, and the human eye
      is very good at finding bodies and faces in it.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    In the story, the Ningen does almost nothing. It surfaces near a ship, it is noticed, and it is
    gone. One popular version has a crew mistaking it for a foreign submarine until its shape gave
    it away as something alive. It does not attack, chase, or take anyone. Its threat is entirely in
    the secrecy around it: the idea that the government knows and is not telling.
  </p>
  <p>
    The more accurate way to describe how the Ningen operates is how it spreads. After 2002 the
    threads went quiet, and then in April 2007 the Japanese occult magazine <em>Mu</em> ran a short
    piece on a map-imagery "mystery": a large white figure with limbs, said to be about nineteen
    metres long, in the sea off the coast of Namibia. The article suggested it might be a
    human-shaped monster called <em>ningen</em> or <em>hitogata</em>. A further <em>Mu</em>
    supplement followed in 2008.
  </p>
  <p>
    From there it moved into manga, a 2008 song voiced by the virtual singer Hatsune Miku, a 2016
    television segment, a 2018 novel, horror videos, and fan art, according to Greenland and
    Hayward's survey. Some later retellings move it north into Arctic waters; the original posts did
    not. Every one of these is a new layer of story, and none of them is new evidence.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>There is no creature here to weaken, so the honest assessment is about the claim.</p>
  <p>
    <strong>There is no witness.</strong> The story starts with an anonymous poster repeating what
    an unnamed insider said. No ship, no crew member, no date of sighting, and no position has ever
    been produced. A claim that cannot be traced to anyone cannot be checked.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The picture is in the wrong ocean.</strong> The most famous image is a blur off Namibia,
    in the South Atlantic, nowhere near the Antarctic whaling grounds where the story is set. A pale
    smudge on map imagery is a weak thing to build a monster on.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The ordinary answers fit.</strong> Marine scientist Andrew Thaler, writing on
    <em>Southern Fried Science</em> in 2010, suggested a modern take on the mermaid myth, the
    face-like underside of large rays, and above all pareidolia: icebergs that happen to look human.
    He also noted he could find no reports earlier than 2007, which shows how far the story's roots
    had already been forgotten by then.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The meaning is the interesting part.</strong> Greenland and Hayward do not ask whether
    the Ningen is real. They argue that its setting in the Southern Ocean, during the disputed years
    of Japanese research whaling, is central to why it appeared at all. On their reading it is a
    story about whales, secrecy, and unease, told as a monster.
  </p>
</Section>

    <p>
      On 11 May 2002, in a 2channel Occult thread about giant fish, an anonymous poster claimed that
      Japan's research whaling in the Antarctic had recorded unexplained "objects" alongside the
      whales. The source, they said, was an insider called Mr F. The things were white, a few tens
      of metres long, and surfaced like whales; some had four limbs and a head. Making them public,
      the poster claimed, would hurt the credibility of research whaling. These are forum claims,
      and they are the only "testimony" the legend has.
    </p>
    <p>
      The poster stopped within a week, but the threads ran on into late 2003, and the legend spread
      through Japanese forums. By January 2010 English and Japanese blogs were describing a white
      humanoid of twenty to thirty metres, seen at night by crews of government whale-research
      ships, and tracing it to "numbered" bulletin boards. Not one of those accounts names a ship, a
      sailor, or a date of sighting.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      In its April 2007 issue, the Japanese occult magazine <em>Mu</em> printed a short piece on a
      map-imagery mystery: a large white figure with limbs, said to be about nineteen metres long,
      in the sea off Namibia. The article floated the idea that it might be the <em>ningen</em> or
      <em>hitogata</em>. Some later accounts date the magazine's coverage to later in 2007, and the
      exact coordinates of the image have not been confirmed. Blogs in 2010 were still reposting a
      map screenshot of the same stretch of sea.
    </p>
    <p>
      The sceptical reading came quickly. In 2010 Andrew Thaler pointed to the mermaid myth, the
      face-like undersides of rays, and pareidolia among the Southern Ocean's icebergs as far more
      likely than a giant unknown animal. A decade later, Greenland and Hayward set aside the
      question of existence entirely and treated the Ningen as media lore tied to the politics of
      whaling. No physical evidence has surfaced in the years between, or since.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Ningen began as one anonymous post on a spring night in 2002, passed on second-hand, about
      a sea most readers would never see. It had no witness and no photograph, and it did not need
      either. It had a good shape — pale, enormous, almost human — and a good secret.
    </p>
    <p>
      That is worth understanding, because it is how a great deal of modern folklore is made. Nobody
      lied about seeing a monster. Someone told a story about what someone else had seen, and
      thousands of people added to it. The real things in this entry are the forum, the magazine,
      the ice, and the cold black water. Respect the last two.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Ask who saw it. Find the first date. And stay sceptical — a story that
      no one witnessed can grow to any size.
    </p>
  </Outro>
