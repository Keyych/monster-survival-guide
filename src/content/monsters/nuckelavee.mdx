<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is late, and you are walking home along an Orkney shore road with no moon. The stars are out,
    so you can see a little: on your left the sea, black and moving; on your right a fresh-water
    loch, flat and still. The road between them is narrow. There is no turning off it.
  </p>
  <p>
    Something big is coming towards you along that road. It is shaped like a horse, but too large,
    and there is something sitting on it that does not sit the way a rider sits. It seems to grow
    out of the horse's back. As it comes closer you see that neither of them has any skin. You
    cannot go left or right, and you have heard that turning your back on a thing like this is the
    worst choice of all.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirty-first journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the
    Nuckelavee, the creature Orkney people feared above all others. It was never photographed or
    caught, and there is only one detailed encounter on record. But it was blamed for a great deal
    of real suffering in the islands, and that is where it is worth your attention.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    Almost everything we know about the Nuckelavee comes from one man. Walter Traill Dennison, who
    signed his letters from West Brough on the island of Sanday, collected stories from older
    islanders, whom he called his old informers. In 1890 and 1891 he printed a series of notes on
    Orkney sea myths in <em>The Scottish Antiquary, or, Northern Notes and Queries</em>. The
    Nuckelavee was the fourth of them. Ten years later, George Douglas reprinted the account in{' '}
    <em>Scottish Fairy and Folk Tales</em> (1901), and most later retellings come from that version.
  </p>
  <p>
    Dennison would not guess at where the name came from, beyond saying it would be obvious to
    readers who knew northern mythology. In plain English, he wrote, it meant Devil of the Sea. He
    also wrote that it was considered unlucky, if not unsafe, to say the name without adding a short
    prayer straight afterwards. Other Orkney spirits were spoken of with something like fondness.
    This one, in his words, was "a monster of unmixed malignity". People mentioned it quietly, and
    only when they had to.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The Nuckelavee is not a creature people claimed to see often. It is a creature people used to
    explain things: a ruined crop, a dead horse, a summer with no rain. Most of the record describes
    what it was blamed for, not what it did.
  </div>
  <p>
    Dennison also places it inside a larger belief about the sea. In his account, only two things
    kept it from emptying Orkney of people altogether: the Mither of the Sea in summer, and in
    winter its own terror of fresh water.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      In Dennison's notes the Sea Mither is a great, kindly power who gives life to every creature
      in the sea. Her rival is Teran, a name he glosses as Orkney dialect for furious anger. Each
      spring, around the equinox gales, the two fight in what islanders called the Vore Tullye, the
      spring struggle, and Teran is bound at the bottom of the sea. Each autumn the worn-out Mither
      loses the Gore Vellye, Teran breaks free, and winter comes. Dennison adds that neither of them
      was ever seen. It is a way of explaining the seasons, and the Nuckelavee lives inside it.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    Dennison gives two descriptions, and they do not fully match. The general one is a rider on a
    horse as terrible as itself, and he notes that some people believed horse and rider were one
    body. The head is like a man's but ten times larger, the mouth sticks out like a pig's snout and
    is enormously wide, and there is no skin anywhere. You can see black blood moving in the veins
    and white sinews working as it moves.
  </p>
  <p>
    The version from Tammas's encounter adds detail. The lower half is a great horse with flaps like
    fins about its legs, a mouth as wide as a whale's, breath like steam from a brewing kettle, and
    a single red eye. The upper half is a huge man with no legs who seems to grow out of the horse's
    back. His arms reach nearly to the ground, and his head, the size of a coil of straw rope about
    three feet across, rolls from shoulder to shoulder as if it might fall off. The blood is black
    as tar in yellow veins, and the sinews are as thick as horse tethers.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      Nearly every vivid detail of the Nuckelavee goes back to a single account, given unwillingly
      by one old man, written down by one collector and then reprinted many times. Modern pictures
      that look certain are copying that account, not adding to it. If you see a confident
      description somewhere, trace it back to 1891 before you trust it.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    In the lore, the Nuckelavee lives in the sea and comes onto land to do harm. Dennison lists what
    it was blamed for: crops spoiled by salt wind or mildew, livestock that fell from the cliffs
    along the shore, and epidemics among people and animals. Its breath was said to be venom that
    withered plants and spread disease. It was also blamed for long droughts, because it disliked
    fresh water and was never known to come ashore while it was raining.
  </p>
  <p>
    It also had a particular grievance. According to Dennison, the burning of seaweed for kelp
    enraged it. It took revenge by striking the horses of Stronsay, which he names as the first
    Orkney island to make kelp, with a deadly disease that spread to every island where kelp was
    made. That disease was called Mortasheen. This is the point where the legend touches recorded
    history. Kelp burning was real, Orkney horses really did die of Mortasheen, and the smoke really
    was resented. All three are covered below.
  </p>
  <p>
    There is no pattern of repeated sightings. The Nuckelavee behaves less like an animal with
    habits and more like a cause people assigned to bad seasons. Its "operation" is the islanders'
    reasoning about hardship: when things went badly wrong at the edge of the sea, something from
    the sea was responsible.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>The tradition gives it two weaknesses, and they are worth stating plainly.</p>
  <p>
    <strong>Fresh water.</strong> This is the one defence the record agrees on. Dennison says it
    feared fresh water and avoided land in the rain. In Tammas's account, a splash of loch water on
    its foreleg makes the horse shy away, and a stream running from the loch to the sea is a line it
    will not cross. In folklore terms the logic holds together: a creature of salt water, kept back
    by fresh.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The Sea Mither.</strong> In summer, Dennison says, she restrained it. As a protection,
    that depends on believing in the whole system of sea myth he recorded. It explains the seasons.
    It does not tell anyone what to do.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The honest weakness is the record itself.</strong> There is one detailed encounter, from
    a reluctant witness whose full name was never given, with no date or place. Nothing was found
    and nothing else was seen. The physical claims, a horse plague and failing crops, have ordinary
    explanations that were known at the time. Mortasheen is the old Scots name for glanders, a
    disease of horses, and Scottish authorities were dealing with it in the 1720s. A real infection
    and an eighteenth-century famine are enough to account for the evidence. A creature is not
    needed.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    One of these cases is the only detailed encounter in the record. The other is where the legend
    meets documented Orkney history: an industry, a disease, and an island that went looking for
    someone to blame.
  </p>

    <p>
      Dennison says he knew an old man who was believed to have met the Nuckelavee and escaped. The
      man did not like talking about it, and the story came out only after a lot of persuading.
      Dennison calls him Tammas and compares him to Burns's Tam o' Shanter, who was also out late
      one night. On a moonless, starlit night, Tammas was walking a road with the sea on one side
      and a deep fresh-water loch on the other when he saw something huge coming towards him. He
      could not leave the road and did not dare turn his back, so he said a short prayer and walked
      slowly forward. He kept to the loch side, because he had heard that the creature hated fresh
      water.
    </p>
    <p>
      When it came level with him, the long arms reached out. Tammas swerved towards the loch and
      one foot went into the water, splashing the horse's foreleg. The horse snorted and shied
      across the road, and the grab missed him. He ran. The creature turned and galloped after him,
      roaring like the sea. Ahead of him was a small stream carrying water from the loch down to the
      sea, and he knew he would be safe if he could get across running water. He jumped, made it to
      the far bank, and collapsed unconscious. The creature was left holding his bonnet. No date or
      exact place is given, and nothing physical was ever found.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      In the eighteenth century, Orkney began burning seaweed to produce kelp, a valuable ash sold
      for making glass and soap. Local history credits James Fea of Whitehall with the first Orkney
      kelp, around 1719. The work was hard, the kilns smoked, and not everyone welcomed it. When
      famine and crop failure hit the islands between 1739 and 1742, people blamed the kelp smoke.
      On 16 May 1742, about fifty protesters attacked kelp kilns on Stronsay, and two Stronsay men
      were later tried and fined. That account says nothing about the Nuckelavee. It records the
      grievance, not a monster.
    </p>
    <p>
      The folklore Dennison collected puts the same resentment in supernatural terms. The kelp smoke
      offended the Nuckelavee, so it struck the horses of Stronsay with Mortasheen, and the disease
      spread to every island that burned kelp. The disease, at least, is well documented. The
      Dictionary of the Scots Language defines mortercheyn, also spelled mortasheen, as glanders. It
      cites a Banff order from 1729 telling owners to keep infected horses shut indoors, and a 1768
      Stenness kirk session entry recording a collection for a poor Harray man whose horse had died
      suddenly of "the Mortasheen". The horses really died. Only the explanation came from the sea.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Nuckelavee is the darkest creature in Orkney's folklore, and it has one witness.
      Everything else in its file is a list of things that went wrong in the islands: blight,
      drought, sick animals, horses lost to plague, and smoke drifting over the shore in a bad year.
      People who lived close to the sea needed a reason for what it seemed to do to them, and they
      gave that reason a face.
    </p>
    <p>
      Treat it with respect, because it is an honest record of hardship. Don't treat it as a field
      guide. The sea, the tide, an unseen rock edge on a moonless night and a contagious disease in
      the stable were the real dangers. They still are, and none of them is put off by fresh water.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Keep to the loch side if you must, but carry a light, know the tide, and
      call the vet. Stay sceptical. The sea is dangerous enough without a devil in it.
    </p>
  </Outro>
</Section>
