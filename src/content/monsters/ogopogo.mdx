<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is a bright summer afternoon in the Okanagan Valley, and you are standing above the lake with
    a phone in your hand. The water is long and narrow, folded between dry brown hills, and it runs
    off to the north and south further than you can see. Out in the middle, a little way off the far
    shore, something dark is lying on the surface.
  </p>
  <p>
    It is long. It seems to be in sections — one dark shape, then a gap, then another — and it does
    not look as though it is drifting the way the light chop is drifting around it. You lift the
    phone and start recording, and the image jumps and blurs every time you zoom. By the time you
    think to steady it against a post, the shape is lower in the water, and then it is gone.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirtieth journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is Ogopogo, the
    lake monster of Okanagan Lake in British Columbia — one of the most filmed cryptids in the
    world, and one whose name was borrowed from a London dance tune. It is also a case where the
    lake carried a far older and very different story long before the monster arrived, and that
    story is not ours to turn into a creature feature.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    Start with the lake. Okanagan Lake runs about 135 kilometres from end to end, only a few
    kilometres wide, and reaches depths of more than two hundred metres. Its shores are the
    traditional territory of the Syilx (Okanagan) people, whose tradition holds the lake —
    kɬúsx̌nítkw in nsyilxcən — to be a living being with its own spirit.
  </p>
  <p>
    In Syilx tradition the lake is home to n̓x̌ax̌aitkʷ — spellings vary between sources, and Tourism
    Kelowna writes it nx̌aʔx̌ʔitkʷ — a spirit of the water. According to the Sncewips Heritage Museum
    of Westbank First Nation, it is a benevolent spirit whose truest form is the water itself, not a
    serpent. Families have long offered tobacco or other gifts when crossing the lake, as thanks and
    to ask for safe passage, and that practice continues. Tourism Kelowna adds that offerings were
    made when crossing near Rattlesnake Island, close to Squally Point, and that the being appears
    in petroglyphs as a creature of the water.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Ogopogo and n̓x̌ax̌aitkʷ are not two names for one animal. One is a sacred teaching about water,
    respect and reciprocity, held by a living Nation. The other is a tourist-era monster. This entry
    is about the second, and is careful not to borrow the first.
  </div>
  <p>
    The monster's name has a much plainer origin. "The Ogo-Pogo," subtitled "The Funny Fox-Trot,"
    was a comic song by lyricist Cumberland Clark and composer Mark Strong, first published in
    London in 1924. Within a few years the palindrome had been attached to the thing people said
    they were seeing in Okanagan Lake, and it stuck. In 1953 a local broadcaster, Gil Seabrook,
    registered a copyright under the name; the rights later passed to the City of Vernon.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      Settler retellings turned the water spirit into a man-eating demon that demanded live
      sacrifice, and some popular accounts still repeat that framing. The Sncewips Heritage Museum
      rejects it plainly: the spirit "does not eat people nor does it need offerings of flesh." The
      museum explains that tobacco, sage and, now and then, a small piece of meat were offered as
      thanks, never out of fear — and that early settlers mistook that gesture for a sacrifice. In
      2021, after objections from Syilx leaders, Vernon's council assigned its Ogopogo copyright to
      the Okanagan Nation Alliance. Okanagan Indian Band Chief Byron Louis had called the city's
      ownership of the name "cultural appropriation."
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    The popular description is consistent in outline. Witnesses describe a long, dark, serpentine
    animal swimming with an undulating motion, usually seen as a line of humps moving through the
    water. Some describe a head like a horse's or a sheep's; the Canadian Encyclopedia notes the
    resemblance to descriptions of the Loch Ness monster. Popular art usually makes it green. One
    recent documentary-maker puts it at up to sixty feet long.
  </p>
  <p>
    Beyond that, the details drift. Some witnesses see humps and no head. Many, as the sceptic
    Benjamin Radford has pointed out, describe nothing more than a straight, featureless dark shape
    lying in the waves — which is a description of something, but not obviously of an animal. One
    widely shared 2022 photograph showed a small orange object with horn-like points, about three
    feet across; a commenter quoted in a review of a later documentary suggested a dead crayfish
    floating upside down.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      On a long, open lake you almost never know how far away a shape is, and without a range you
      cannot know its size. A duck at a kilometre and a whale at five can look the same. The single
      best film of Ogopogo appears to be exactly this mistake. Before you estimate length, find
      something of known size beside it — a boat, a buoy, a stretch of shoreline.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The pattern of a sighting barely changes from decade to decade. Someone on the shore, or on a
    boat, sees a dark shape or a row of humps out on the water, often on a calm day. It seems to
    move against the waves or faster than the current. It submerges or simply stops being visible.
    If a camera is present, the footage is distant and unsteady.
  </p>
  <p>
    The record is long and almost entirely visual. The earliest settler stories go back to the
    nineteenth century; Tourism Kelowna also records that Susan Allison, a settler, was sure in 1872
    that she had seen a dinosaur in the lake. In August 1968 Arthur Folden shot about a minute of
    home-movie footage of something large and lifelike on calm water — still the best-known film. In
    2011 Richard Huls filmed two dark shapes from a winery in West Kelowna and said of them, "It was
    not going with the waves." Photographs and videos have kept arriving since.
  </p>
  <p>
    What has never arrived is a body. In 1991 an expedition financed by Japan's Nippon Television
    took a remotely operated vehicle and a miniature submarine down to the deepest part of the lake.
    It found no animal, no carcass and no bones. A creature large enough to be seen from shore,
    breeding in a closed lake for well over a century, would be expected to leave remains somewhere.
    None has ever been produced.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    There is no monster here to fight, so the honest assessment is of the evidence — and the
    evidence is weaker than the volume of footage suggests.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The lake makes its own humps.</strong> Radford notes that the geography of Okanagan Lake
    produces long, unusual waves that can look exactly like a string of humps. A row of humps
    travelling in a straight line on a long, narrow lake is exactly what such a wave train looks
    like.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The lake is full of logs.</strong> Decades of the timber trade have left large numbers
    of logs floating just under the surface. The Huls video, on Radford's reading, shows two
    straight, dark forms side by side with no head or body shape at all — which fits floating timber
    far better than it fits a serpent.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The best film shrinks under analysis.</strong> In 2005 Radford and John Kirk of the
    British Columbia Scientific Cryptozoology Club examined the Folden film for a National
    Geographic Channel programme. They concluded it shows a real animal, but that its size had been
    greatly overestimated — most likely a waterfowl or beaver too far away to identify.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The tradition is not evidence.</strong> Supporters often cite Indigenous stories as
    proof the animal is ancient. Even Radford, from the sceptical side, argues the old stories
    describe a water spirit rather than a literal lake monster — and the Syilx accounts above say
    the same thing in their own terms. Using a sacred tradition as cryptozoological evidence
    misreads it twice.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of that makes the witnesses liars. Kirk considers the Okanagan footage more plentiful and
    of better quality than anything he has seen from Loch Ness. Plentiful footage of distant,
    ambiguous shapes is still distant and ambiguous.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    The first case is the oldest settler story, retold for well over a century and read very
    differently by the people whose lake it is. The second is the best film ever taken, and what
    happened when it was examined.
  </p>

    <p>
      The story is usually told like this. In the 1850s — Tourism Kelowna gives 1855 — a settler
      named John McDougall was crossing Okanagan Lake by canoe with his horses swimming behind on
      ropes. Partway across, something dragged the horses down. McDougall cut the ropes to stop the
      canoe from being pulled under with them. It is often cited as the first settler account of the
      creature turning aggressive.
    </p>
    <p>
      It is a second-hand story with no date that everyone agrees on, and it has been told and
      retold for a very long time. In a documentary following a 2023 expedition, reviewed by Judith
      Tarr, the Syilx artist and museum docent Coralee Miller offers a plainer reading: McDougall
      most likely ran into a strong current he did not understand. Swimming horses tied to a small
      boat on a big, cold lake is a dangerous arrangement with no monster involved. The same story
      also fed the settler idea that the lake demanded sacrifice — the misunderstanding the Sncewips
      Heritage Museum has since set out to correct.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      On a sunny August day in 1968, Arthur Folden filmed about a minute of home-movie footage of
      something large and apparently alive moving on the calm surface of Okanagan Lake. For decades
      it was held up as the strongest film evidence for Ogopogo, and it is still the footage most
      often cited. In 1991 a Nippon Television expedition searched the deepest part of the lake with
      a remotely operated vehicle and a miniature submarine and found nothing to match it.
    </p>
    <p>
      In 2005, for the National Geographic Channel programme <em>Is It Real?</em>, Benjamin Radford
      and the cryptozoologist John Kirk examined the film together. They agreed it shows a real
      animal rather than a wave or a log — but concluded that its size had been greatly
      overestimated, and that it was probably a waterfowl or a beaver, too far from the camera to
      identify. That is where the case rests: an honest film of something real, made extraordinary
      by a distance nobody could measure.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      Ogopogo is a monster named after a fox-trot, given a copyright, and filmed more often than
      almost any creature in this guide — and in all that time the lake has given up nothing but
      footage. The humps are probably waves, the long dark shapes probably logs, and the best film
      probably a bird a long way off. None of that requires anyone to have lied.
    </p>
    <p>
      The lake had a story long before it had a monster, and it still does. That story belongs to
      the Syilx, and it is about water — respecting it, giving thanks for it, and remembering that
      what happens upstream reaches everyone downstream. It was never a creature to be survived.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Wear the lifejacket. Find something to measure the shape against. And
      leave the lake's older story to the people who keep it.
    </p>
  </Outro>
