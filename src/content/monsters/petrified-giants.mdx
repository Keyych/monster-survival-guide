<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is a Saturday in October 1869, and you have paid your money at the flap of a tent on a farm
    outside the village of Cardiff, in Onondaga County, New York. The queue behind you is long and
    still growing. Inside, at the bottom of a pit dug for a well, a man is lying on his back in the
    wet earth. He is more than ten feet long. He is grey, and he is stone, and his face looks as
    though he died in some discomfort.
  </p>
  <p>
    People around you are murmuring about the giants of Genesis. Others are saying it is a statue,
    hundreds of years old. Nobody is saying the obvious thing, because the obvious thing is that a
    farmer has found a miracle exactly where he was having a well dug, and is charging you to look
    at it.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirty-seventh journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide, and it is about a hoax.
    The Cardiff Giant was carved gypsum, buried on purpose and dug up on cue. It was followed by a
    run of imitators, the best known being Colorado's "Solid Muldoon" in 1877. There is no creature
    in this entry. There is only a crowd, a showman, and the question of how much you are willing to
    pay to believe something.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The man behind it was George Hull, a New York tobacconist and cigar maker, and by most accounts
    a committed atheist. The story he told, and that later histories repeat, is that in the
    mid-1860s he argued with a Methodist revivalist in Iowa over whether the Bible should be read
    literally. Accounts differ on the exact year. What Hull took away from it was the thought that
    people would believe almost anything if it was presented as proof.
  </p>
  <p>
    He bought a block of gypsum near Fort Dodge, Iowa, reportedly claiming it was for a statue of
    Abraham Lincoln, and had it shipped to Chicago, where stonecutters carved it into a reclining
    man during the late summer of 1868. In November of that year he and an accomplice buried it
    behind the barn of his cousin, William C. "Stub" Newell, at Cardiff. One account puts the cost
    of the venture at over $2,600. Then they waited about a year.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The petrified giant is not a legend that grew. It is a product that was manufactured, buried,
    and launched — and the paper trail of its making survives because the people who made it
    eventually argued over the money.
  </div>
  <p>
    Hull was working with an appetite that already existed. Reports of petrified bodies were a minor
    craze in American papers by the 1860s; Mark Twain had mocked the fashion in October 1862 with a
    fake report of a petrified man in Nevada's <em>Territorial Enterprise</em>, and many papers
    reprinted it without noticing the joke. Hull's innovation was to give the public something it
    could actually stand in front of.
  </p>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The Cardiff Giant did not go away when it was exposed. It changed hands among exhibitors for
      decades and was eventually owned by a Des Moines publisher, who in 1947 sold it to the
      Farmers' Museum in Cooperstown, New York. One account puts the price at $30,000. It is still
      there, shown under a tent made to resemble the one on Newell's farm. P. T. Barnum's copy of it
      survives too, and is believed to be in a mechanical-amusements museum near Detroit.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="What You Are Looking At">
  <StatRow

  <p>
    The Cardiff Giant is a naked male figure carved from a single block of gypsum, lying on its back
    with the right hand across the lower abdomen and the left arm pressed behind it. The 1869
    exhibition pamphlet gave the length from the top of the head to the instep as ten feet three
    inches; later accounts put it at around 10 feet and about 3,000 pounds.
  </p>
  <p>
    The Solid Muldoon, eight years later, was smaller and stranger: about seven and a half feet
    long, reportedly around 600 pounds, brownish, with oversized hands and feet and a short tail at
    the base of the spine. It was not carved at all but cast — accounts describe a mortar of rock
    dust, clay, plaster, ground bone and other organic matter, fired to harden it and give it an
    antique look.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      Fossilisation replaces tissue slowly, mineral by mineral, and almost never preserves an entire
      soft body in recognisable form. A whole human figure in stone, with an intact face and relaxed
      limbs, is the signature of a sculptor, not of geology. The scientists of 1869 knew this; the
      crowds paid anyway.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    Every petrified-giant hoax ran on the same four stages. First, the object was made in secret,
    far from where it would be found — the Cardiff figure in Chicago, the Muldoon reportedly in a
    kiln in northern Pennsylvania. Second, it was buried and left long enough for the ground to look
    undisturbed. Third, it was "discovered" by people with a plausible reason to be digging: well
    workers at Cardiff, a local man and his son in Colorado.
  </p>
  <p>
    Fourth, and most important, it was monetised immediately. Newell had a tent up and was charging
    admission within days of the find, at first 25 cents and soon 50. A syndicate of local
    businessmen, including David Hannum, then bought into the giant and moved it to Syracuse, and
    accounts of what they paid run well into the tens of thousands of dollars. The show travelled,
    and the controversy travelled with it, because controversy sold tickets.
  </p>
  <p>
    The decisive move came from P. T. Barnum. When the owners would not sell or lease him the giant,
    he had a copy made and exhibited it in New York City as the genuine article. The owners went to
    court for an injunction; by one account the judge wanted proof that their own giant was genuine,
    and the matter went no further. No reported case has ever been found. The phrase "there's a
    sucker born every minute" is often attached to this quarrel, but no record of who said it, or
    whether anyone did, has been found.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>The petrified giants have exactly one weakness, and it is the same one every time: people.</p>
  <p>
    <strong>The science was against them from the first week.</strong> State Geologist James Hall
    and others concluded that the Cardiff figure was a carved statue, not a fossil. Many still
    thought it ancient, but in November 1869 Yale palaeontologist Othniel C. Marsh examined it and
    pronounced it "of very recent origin, and a most decided humbug" — noting, among other things,
    chisel marks that would not have survived long burial.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The numbers were against them.</strong> Quarrymen, carvers, carters and a farmer all had
    to keep quiet, and the more people a hoax needs, the more people can talk. Hull confessed in the
    press on 10 December 1869, less than two months after the dig.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The partners were against each other.</strong> The Muldoon was finished not by a
    laboratory but by a disgruntled insider who went to the press, naming Hull as its maker. Hoaxes
    that require several people to share profits tend to end in exactly this way.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The audience was not.</strong> That is the honest problem. Even after the experts had
    spoken, people kept paying to see the Cardiff Giant, and Barnum's copy reportedly drew larger
    crowds than the original. Being exposed did not stop a petrified giant from earning money. It
    only changed what people thought they were paying for.
  </p>
</Section>

  </p>

    <p>
      On Saturday 16 October 1869, two men, Gideon Emmons and Henry Nichols, were digging a well on
      William Newell's farm near Cardiff, in the town of Lafayette, when one of them struck stone.
      By the exhibition pamphlet's account, published that same year, they dug on and uncovered the
      face of a reclining figure. Within days it was under canvas and drawing paying crowds. The
      pamphlet itself sets out the learned debate: Dr John F. Boynton thought it a statue made by
      early Jesuit missionaries; Professor James Hall wrote plainly that "It is a statue, cut in
      gypsum"; others insisted it was a true petrifaction.
    </p>
    <p>
      By mid-December the affair had become a commercial war. The New-York Tribune of 15 December
      1869 carried advertisements for two giants at once: Wood's Museum promoting "the great
      Onondaga Giant," with a sworn statement that it was not a cast, and the Cardiff Giant's own
      exhibitors announcing that the figure then at the State Geological Hall in Albany would open
      at Apollo Hall from Monday 20 December. The advertisement does not say whether the Wood's
      giant was Barnum's copy. Hull had already confessed. The owners' attempt to stop Barnum's
      rival in court was heard but, as a Library of Congress legal researcher found, apparently
      never reported as a decided case.
    </p>

    <p>
      In September 1877, William Conant and his son reported finding a petrified figure in the hills
      near Beulah, Colorado. Within weeks it was on show and in the papers. The Dodge City Times of
      27 October 1877 described a long, powerfully built man lying on his back, with very large
      hands and feet and a short tail, about seven feet five inches long, and passed on the claim
      that scientists dated him to a million years ago. Barnum's name was attached to it from the
      start; later accounts say he offered $20,000 for it. Not everyone was impressed: the Cheyenne
      Daily Leader of 9 October joked about a visitor who called it poorly chiselled statuary
      because four of its toes were the same length.
    </p>
    <p>
      Accounts of the exposure differ in detail, but agree on the shape. An insider who felt cheated
      of his share went to the press and named George Hull, the Cardiff Giant's maker, as the man
      behind it, working with Conant. The figure had reportedly been cast and kiln-fired in
      Pennsylvania, shipped west by rail and buried for months before it was found. Even at the
      time, rival stories circulated — one Vermont paper in November 1877 printed a Denver assayer's
      claim that a local stonecutter had made it — and later sources disagree on whether the name
      came from a popular song or the strongman William Muldoon. The original's whereabouts are
      unknown; a replica was buried near the site in 1976.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The tent on Stub Newell's farm is gone, but the giant is still lying in Cooperstown, under a
      canvas built to look like the original, and people still pay to see him. That is not a failure
      of the exposure. It is the most honest thing about him. He was always a show about believing,
      and the show still works once you know the trick.
    </p>
    <p>
      George Hull spent over two thousand dollars and a year of waiting to prove that people would
      believe almost anything placed in front of them with enough confidence. He was right, and then
      he did it again in Colorado. The lesson is not that people in 1869 were foolish. It is that
      the same arrangement — a convenient discovery, a ticket price, a respectable expert, a rival
      who calls the other a fake — still works on everyone.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Ask who is selling the tickets. Look for the chisel marks. And stay
      sceptical — the bigger the marvel, the smaller the print.
    </p>
  </Outro>
</Section>
