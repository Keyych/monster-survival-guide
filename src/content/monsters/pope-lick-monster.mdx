<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is a warm night on the eastern edge of Louisville, Kentucky, and you are standing at the foot
    of a railway bridge. Above you, the iron trestle runs out over Pope Lick Creek and the road
    beneath it, black against the sky, much higher than it looked from the car. Someone in your
    group has told you the story on the way here: something lives under this bridge — a man from the
    waist up and a goat below — and it calls people out onto the tracks.
  </p>
  <p>
    There is a fence, and signs, and a path worn around them. Your friends are already talking about
    walking out to the middle. The creek is very quiet. Nobody has mentioned the timetable, because
    nobody here knows it.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the thirty-third journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject is the Pope
    Lick Monster — a goat-man that has never been seen, attached to a bridge that has repeatedly
    killed people. It is the clearest case in this guide of a legend whose danger is entirely real,
    and entirely ordinary. Read it as a warning about a railway, not a creature.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The trestle is real and old. It carries the Norfolk Southern freight line over Pope Lick Creek
    and South Pope Lick Road near Fisherville, in eastern Jefferson County, and local accounts date
    it to the late 1800s. It stands roughly 90 feet above the ground and runs for somewhere around
    750 feet — published figures differ slightly — with no railings and no place to get out of the
    way of a train.
  </p>
  <p>
    The monster has no such paper trail. There is no historical event behind it, no first witness,
    no date of origin. The Parklands of Floyds Fork, writing in 2011, described the stories as
    having circulated for more than three generations, mostly as the setting for teenage dares.
    Brian Dunning of Skeptoid, examining the legend in 2023, found it vague in every detail and
    noted that goat-men are told about in many other places too, from Maryland to Texas to another
    corner of Kentucky.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Most legends grow out of something that happened. This one runs the other way: the story came
    first, and the real deaths followed it onto the bridge.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      The origin stories contradict each other freely. In one, he is a circus sideshow performer who
      escaped when the circus train crashed — or, in another telling, when lightning struck it. In
      another, he is a farmer who sacrificed goats to the Devil and became one. Other versions offer
      a disfigured chemist or a hermit in a shack. None is attached to a name, a date, or a record.
      That looseness is typical of a story passed between teenagers rather than handed down by a
      community.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <StatRow

  <p>
    Descriptions of the creature agree only loosely. Most give it a man's upper body and a goat's
    legs, with horns; some add a pale, alabaster face. In other versions it is more sheep than goat
    — locals have called it the "Sheepman." Nobody has photographed it, and no account names a
    witness who could be questioned.
  </p>
  <p>
    What you can describe precisely is the bridge. It is a long trestle carrying working freight
    trains, with no room on it to step aside. Below is a drop that the deputy coroner, after a death
    in 2016, estimated at 80 to 100 feet. At the base there is a chain-link fence and posted
    warnings. Residents told reporters that same year that trains pass roughly every half hour.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      The thing that kills people on this bridge is a freight train, arriving on an active line
      where there is no room to stand aside and no time to reach the end. Many visitors, a local
      historian told WAVE 3 News, wrongly believe the line is no longer in use. It is. Do not walk
      onto the trestle, for any reason, at any hour.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    In the legend, the goat-man works by lure. He is said to mimic familiar voices or to hypnotise,
    drawing people out along the tracks until a train arrives. Other versions have him attack with
    an axe, or drop from the ironwork onto cars passing on the road beneath.
  </p>
  <p>
    The real mechanism is simpler, and it is the same every time. People go to the trestle because
    of the story, usually at night and usually in a group. They pass the fence, walk out onto the
    span, and are partway across when a train comes. With no railings and no refuges, the choices
    are to run for an end they cannot reach, or to hang from the side of the bridge. People have
    been struck. People have fallen. A few have clung on long enough to be rescued.
  </p>
  <p>
    That pattern has repeated across decades — in the 1980s, the 1990s, in 2000, in 2016 and in
    2019. The goat-man supplies the reason to go. The railway supplies everything else.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    The creature has every weakness a legend can have, and none of them makes the bridge any safer.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>It cannot exist.</strong> As Dunning points out, a goat-human hybrid is biologically
    impossible; the two lineages parted around 100 million years ago. There is no carcass, no track,
    no photograph and no named witness.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Its story has no fixed shape.</strong> Goat or sheep, circus escapee or devil-farmer,
    voice-mimic or axe-killer — the details change with the teller, which is what you would expect
    of a dare rather than a sighting.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Its real power is publicity.</strong> Dunning argues that the legend became famous
    largely through a 1988 short film and the furious local argument about it. Each new death has
    renewed the story. Every retelling — including this one — risks doing the same, which is why
    this entry says plainly: the only thing to survive here is the train, and the only way to
    survive it is to stay on the ground.
  </p>
  <p>
    The fence is a weakness of a different kind. Local reporting has found it bent open, its signs
    hard to read at night, and a railway police manager confirmed that trespassers can be cited or
    arrested for a misdemeanour. Neither the law nor the fence has stopped people going. Only the
    decision not to go does.
  </p>
</Section>

    The first case is how the legend became famous, and how the railway responded. The second is
    what the legend has cost. Victims are not named here; their families have suffered enough from
    this story.
  </p>

    <p>
      By the late 1980s the trestle already had a body count. The Parklands records a 17-year-old
      killed by a train in February 1987, and a 19-year-old who died that May of injuries suffered a
      year earlier, when he jumped from the bridge to escape a train. Into that setting came
      <em>The Legend of the Pope Lick Monster</em>, a 16-minute black-and-white horror short by
      Louisville filmmaker Ron Schildknecht, shot at the trestle. In one scene a teenager survives a
      train by hanging from a railroad tie — a feat the Parklands calls physically impossible.
    </p>
    <p>
      Parents and the railway were alarmed that the film would draw more teenagers onto the bridge.
      Dunning reports that Norfolk Southern's lawyers objected and that the argument filled the{' '}
      <em>Courier-Journal</em> for weeks; at the premiere, a railway statement warned that anyone
      caught on the trestle would be prosecuted. Afterwards a tall chain-link fence went up at the
      base, with danger and keep-off signs. When the film was later posted online, Schildknecht
      added an epilogue with a blunt instruction: "Stay off the damn trestle."
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      The fence did not end it. Local fire chief Sean Dreisbach told WAVE 3 News he had personally
      seen more than half a dozen people killed or injured by trains on the trestle. In 1993 he
      helped rescue a teenage girl found clinging to the side after a train passed. In 1994 a man
      was killed when his ATV overturned on the track and trapped him; in 2000 a 19-year-old fell to
      his death after meeting a train. "A train comes with little to no warning," Dreisbach said.
    </p>
    <p>
      In April 2016 a 26-year-old woman from Ohio, in Louisville for a haunted tour, heard about the
      legend and went onto the tracks with her boyfriend. A train surprised them; unable to reach
      the end, they hung from the side. He survived. She was killed, and the deputy coroner
      estimated her fall at 80 to 100 feet. In late May 2019 a 15-year-old girl was killed and
      another seriously injured. Her parents sued Norfolk Southern, alleging that the trestle was
      unsecured and too easy to reach. WHAS11 found part of the fence bent back for weeks; the
      railway said there had been no major changes to track safety.
    </p>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Pope Lick Monster has never been seen, and never will be. It is a campfire story, told at
      the foot of a bridge to make someone brave enough, or foolish enough, to walk out onto it. As
      a piece of Louisville folklore it is harmless enough. As a reason to climb a fence onto an
      active railway it has been fatal, again and again.
    </p>
    <p>
      So go back to that warm night at the foot of the trestle. The creek is quiet, your friends are
      talking about the middle of the span, and nobody knows when the next train is due. That is the
      whole of the danger. There is nothing under the bridge. There is something coming along the
      line.
    </p>
    <p>
      End of journal entry. Stay off the trestle. Look from the ground. And if anyone you know is
      planning to go out there for the legend, tell them what actually happens to people who do.
    </p>
  </Outro>
</Section>
