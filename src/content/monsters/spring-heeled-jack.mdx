<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    It is a cold night in the winter of 1838, in one of the half-built suburbs crawling out from the
    edge of London. There is a knock at the door. The man outside wears a dark cloak and announces,
    in the voice of authority, that he is a police officer — they have caught Spring-heeled Jack at
    last, and he needs a light. You bring a candle to the door, because why would you not.
  </p>
  <p>
    He lets the cloak fall. There is a spurt of pale blue flame toward your face, a grab at your
    collar, a tearing at your dress with hands that feel cold and hard as metal. You scream; someone
    drags you back inside; and by the time anyone reaches the street he is gone — over a wall, into
    the dark, faster than a man has any right to move.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the twenty-third journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject: Spring-heeled
    Jack — the leaping terror of Victorian London. And like the Chupacabra, this is an entry where
    the guide owes you honesty, because Jack is one of the few monsters we can watch being built in
    public, out of real fear, real assaults, and a newspaper trade that had just discovered how well
    terror sold.
  </p>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    Jack has a documented childhood. The first reports cluster in the London suburbs in 1837 — a
    cloaked figure that sprang on lone pedestrians, mostly women, then vanished. It was{' '}
    <em>The Times</em>, in the winter of 1837–38, that gave the attacker the name that stuck:
    Spring-heeled Jack, a single tidy label for a scatter of nocturnal frights across the city's
    northern and eastern fringes.
  </p>
  <p>
    The panic peaked in January 1838 inside the Mansion House itself, where the Lord Mayor of
    London, Sir John Cowan, read out an anonymous complaint and then — over the following days —
    displayed a growing pile of letters from terrified suburbs. Cowan, to his credit, was openly
    sceptical of the supernatural half of it. But the name was already loose, and it behaved exactly
    as a good name does: it gathered everything.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    Most monsters hide in the dark of the past. This one was assembled in public — in the
    newspapers, with named witnesses, a sceptical Lord Mayor, and a stack of letters we can still
    read. That is not a flaw in the story. That is the story.
  </div>
  <div class="lore-box">
    <p>
      By the 1860s Jack had leapt off the police page and onto the stage and the serial. The penny
      dreadful <em>Spring-heeled Jack — the Terror of London</em> ran in weekly episodes, and
      theatre melodramas gave him springs in his boots, horns, and bat-like wings. The folklorist
      David Clarke traces almost everything "impossible" about Jack — the rooftop bounds, the
      flight, the devilish costume — to this fiction rather than to the first witness statements.
      The name even drifted into street slang for any cloaked footpad.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

  <p>
    There is no single Spring-heeled Jack, and the two versions do not match. The first is the
    figure of the 1838 depositions: a man in a cloak, roughly human-sized, who posed as an official
    or lurked in an unlit passage, produced a flash of flame, clawed at his victim, and fled over a
    wall. Cruel, frightening, and entirely physical — a person committing assaults.
  </p>
  <p>
    The second is the creature of the penny dreadfuls and the stage: a caped acrobat with
    spring-loaded boots, glowing eyes, horns, and leathery wings, bounding across rooftops and
    breathing fire at will. This one sold for a penny a week. It is the one most people picture —
    and it is the one that never filed a deposition or stood in a dock.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      When someone describes "Spring-heeled Jack," establish which one they mean. If it is the
      cloaked prowler at a doorway or in an alley, you are looking at a dangerous man with a costume
      and a conjuror's trick — something a locked door and a lit street can defend against. If it is
      the fire-breathing, wall-leaping acrobat, you are looking at thirty years of theatre and cheap
      print, and the only thing it can hurt is your judgement.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The reported method is consistent and very human. Jack isolates: he waits in a dark passage, or
    he comes to a door and borrows the authority of a policeman to get it opened. He creates panic
    fast — a sudden spurt of blue-and-white flame to the face, which blinds and terrifies — then he
    grabs, claws, and runs before anyone can give chase or fix a clear description.
  </p>
  <p>
    The "impossible" leap is doing a lot of work in the legend, and most of it arrives after the
    fact. In the earliest statements he runs and climbs like a fit, frightened man escaping a scene.
    The rooftop bounds and the vanishing come later, from retellings and from fiction — the gap
    between what a panicked witness saw at night and what a serial needed him to do by the next
    installment.
  </p>
  <p>
    What is real here is a string of genuine assaults on real women, and a handful of later frights
    like the Aldershot sentries in 1877, where guards challenged a cloaked figure, fired, and hit
    nothing. Real fear, real victims, real pranksters — stitched by a single name into one
    impossible biography.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    This is the entry where the honest assessment is the point, so plainly: Spring-heeled Jack has
    never survived contact with a primary source.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The creature collapses.</strong> In sixty-seven years of reports, no body, no capture,
    and no conviction. The one man tried for an Alsop-style attack, Thomas Millbank, walked free
    precisely because he could not breathe fire — the "supernatural" detail was what a human
    defendant could not supply.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The powers collapse.</strong> The flame is a phosphorus-and-conjuror's trick well within
    1838 means; the fantastic leaps belong to the stage and the serials, not the depositions. Strip
    the fiction and you are left with a prowler, a costume, and a flash of light.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The single name collapses.</strong> The Mansion House postbag shows the real mechanism —
    one catchy label acting as a magnet for every unrelated prank, assault, and fright across a
    nervous city, amplified by a press that had found a story it loved.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of that makes the assaults imaginary or the fear unreasonable. It means the thing to defend
    against was a man, and the thing to be sceptical of is the biography the newspapers gave him.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two cases bracket the panic of 1838: the attack that fixed Jack's image in the public
    mind, and the Mansion House session that shows how a city turned a scatter of frights into a

    <p>
      On the night of 19–20 February 1838, Jane Alsop answered the door of Bearbind Cottage, near
      Old Ford in Bow, to a man who claimed to be a police officer and shouted for a light — they
      had caught Spring-heeled Jack. When she brought a candle, he threw off his cloak, spurted
      blue-and- white flame toward her face, and tore at her gown and neck with claws she described
      as cold and hard, "of some metallic substance." Her sisters hauled her back indoors and bolted
      the door.
    </p>
    <p>
      The attack was reported in <em>The Times</em> on 2 March 1838 as "the Late Outrage at Old
      Ford," and it became the template for every Jack that followed. A plasterer named Thomas
      Millbank, who had drunkenly boasted at the Morgan's Arms that he was the attacker, was
      arrested and tried at Lambeth Street. He was acquitted — Jane swore her assailant had breathed
      fire, Millbank plainly could not, and the one "impossible" detail of her account was exactly
      what cleared a very possible man.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

    <p>
      On 9 January 1838, the Lord Mayor of London, Sir John Cowan, read out at the Mansion House an
      anonymous letter signed "a resident of Peckham." It alleged a wager among wealthy men that had
      sent one of their number into the villages around London in three disguises — a ghost, a bear,
      and a devil — to frighten the inhabitants. Cowan was openly sceptical of the supernatural
      element, judging the wilder feats exaggerated and "impossible."
    </p>
    <p>
      Scepticism did not matter. The session opened a floodgate: over the following days the Lord
      Mayor displayed a swelling pile of letters from Hammersmith, Stockwell, Brixton, Camberwell,
      Vauxhall, Lewisham, and Blackheath, reporting women thrown into fits and clawed by a cloaked
      figure. This is the panic in miniature — one memorable name acting as a magnet for every
      unrelated prank, assault, and night-terror across a jittery city, with the national press
      carrying it all the next morning.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>
