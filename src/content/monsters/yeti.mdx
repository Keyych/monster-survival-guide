<Section heading="If You're Reading This">
  <p>
    You are somewhere above twenty thousand feet, on a glacier west of Everest, and the air has
    about half the oxygen your body was built for. The light is blinding. Your head aches in the
    particular way that altitude aches. And in the snow ahead of you is a line of tracks — large,
    deep, and shaped, you could swear, like the print of a bare human foot far too big to be human.
  </p>
  <p>
    You have no tape measure. The sun is already softening the edges. In an hour the prints will be
    wider than they are now; by tomorrow they will be shapeless ovals. Whatever you decide they are,
    you have to decide it here, cold and oxygen-starved, standing over the single most famous piece
    of evidence any monster in this guide has ever produced.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is the eleventh journal entry of the Monster Survival Guide. The subject: the Yeti — the
    being the Sherpa named long before the West arrived, and the West promptly renamed the Abominable
    Snowman. It is also an entry where the honest answer and the exciting answer pull in opposite
    directions, and we are going to side with honesty.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Where It Comes From">
  <p>
    The Yeti does not begin with a cryptozoologist. It begins in the oral tradition of the Sherpa and
    the Tibetan communities of the high Himalaya, who distinguished between several beings long before
    any outsider went looking. Among the names recorded are the <em>meh-teh</em> — roughly a
    man-sized wild creature — the <em>dzu-teh</em>, a larger cattle-killer, and the{' '}
    <em>mi-go</em>, the "wild man" of the snows. These were not one monster. They were a vocabulary.
  </p>
  <p>
    The famous English name is an accident. In 1921, after the British Everest reconnaissance
    reported strange tracks, the journalist Henry Newman — writing under the byline "Kim" for the
    Calcutta <em>Statesman</em> — interviewed the returning porters about the term{' '}
    <em>metoh-kangmi</em>. He rendered <em>metoh</em> not as something like "man-bear" but as
    "filthy," then reached for the punchier "abominable." Newman later admitted the whole thing
    "seemed such a joyous creation" that he sent it round the papers. The monster, in other words,
    got its headline before it got a hearing.
  </p>
  <div class="pull-quote">
    The Sherpa had three careful names for what lived in those mountains. A newspaper flattened them
    into two words that have misled everyone for a century. The first thing to survive about the Yeti
    is the translation.
  </div>
  <p>
    From there the legend climbed with the expeditions. A 1925 account from the Greek photographer
    A. N. Tombazi, relayed to the Royal Geographical Society, described a dark figure on the
    Annapurna side, upright, that had vanished by the time he reached the spot — where he found only
    footprints. Each decade added relics, photographs, and claims, and each one pulled the Yeti
    further from the measured tradition it came from and closer to the thing the West wanted it to be.
  </p>
</Section>

  <p>
    There is no single agreed Yeti, because the tradition never described one. The reports converge
    on a large, shaggy, upright creature covered in reddish-brown or dark hair, broad through the
    shoulders, that walks on two legs and leaves a wide, five-toed, humanlike track in snow. Height
    estimates balloon with the retelling, which is the first clue that you are dealing with a story
    rather than a measured animal.
  </p>
  <p>
    What the evidence keeps resolving into is a bear. The Himalayan brown bear (<em>Ursus arctos
    isabellinus</em>) and the Tibetan blue bear (<em>U. a. pruinosus</em>) both rear onto their hind
    legs to look around, stand taller than a person when they do, and — crucially — place a hind paw
    into the impression left by a forepaw. The result is an elongated, overlapping print that reads
    to a startled eye as a single enormous barefoot human track.
  </p>
  <div class="warning-box">
    <div class="warn-label">⚠ Critical Field Note</div>
    <p>
      Before you decide you are looking at a Yeti, establish what the snow has done to the evidence.
      A print in sun-warmed snow grows for hours after it is made; overlapping tracks melt together;
      a bear's natural gait leaves a humanlike line. The classic Yeti footprint is less a portrait of
      an animal than a photograph of how much melting has happened since it walked past.
    </p>
  </div>
</Section>

<Section heading="How It Operates">
  <p>
    The reported pattern is sparse and consistent: tracks far more often than a figure, a figure far
    more often than a clear look, and almost never contact. Herders report livestock losses in high
    pasture; climbers report a dark shape on a ridge that is gone by the time they close the distance;
    expeditions bring back prints in the snow and, occasionally, a relic held in a monastery.
  </p>
  <p>
    That profile matches a shy, high-altitude omnivore avoiding people — which is exactly how a bear
    behaves. The dzu-teh of Sherpa tradition, the larger cattle-raider, maps neatly onto a brown bear
    taking livestock. What does not happen, in any verified record, is the thing the horror version
    promises: a stalking, a confrontation, a human kill. The Yeti's "behaviour" is mostly the
    behaviour of tracks in snow and stories told afterward.
  </p>
  <p>
    What is genuinely dangerous up here is the setting. People die on these slopes every season — to
    altitude sickness, cold, avalanche, and crevasse — and a frightening legend in a lethal landscape
    is a bad combination, because it points your attention at the wrong threat.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="An Honest Assessment of Its Weaknesses">
  <p>
    As with the goat-sucker, the honest assessment is the whole point, so plainly: the Yeti has never
    survived laboratory contact either.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The name collapses.</strong> "Abominable Snowman" is a documented mistranslation, and the
    single tradition it implies never existed — the Sherpa described several distinct beings.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The tracks collapse.</strong> Snow enlarges and merges prints, and a bear's overlapping
    fore-and-hind gait produces the humanlike footprint on its own. Even the best photographs are
    measurement problems before they are mysteries.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The specimens collapse.</strong> When alleged Yeti hair, bone, tooth, skin, and
    droppings were genetically sequenced, they came back as bears — and in one case a dog. Not one
    was an unknown species.
  </p>
  <p>
    None of that makes the mountain safe or the tradition foolish. It means the creature to respect
    is a bear, the danger to prepare for is the altitude, and the thing to be sceptical of is the
    headline.
  </p>
</Section>

<Section heading="Two Cases on the Record">
  <p>
    These two cases bookend the modern Yeti: the photograph that launched the search, and the study
    that, for most scientists, closed it.
  </p>

  >
    <p>
      In 1951, during the Everest reconnaissance, mountaineers Eric Shipton and Michael Ward were
      scouting an alternative route when they came upon a line of large prints in the snow of the
      Menlung Glacier, near the Nepal–Tibet border. Having no means of measuring them, Shipton laid an
      ice axe and a booted foot beside the clearest print for scale and took his now-famous
      photographs. He noted that most of the trail had already blurred in the thaw, with only one
      print still showing distinct toes.
    </p>
    <p>
      That single sharp image became the foundation of half a century of Yeti research. It is also,
      on closer reading, a textbook illustration of why snow tracks are unreliable: the prints were
      melting as they were photographed. Decades later, researcher Daniel Taylor — who spent much of
      his life investigating the mystery — concluded that the Menlung tracks were most plausibly made
      by an Asian black bear, whose overlapping paw prints and thumb-like digit can account for the
      humanlike shape. The photograph is real; the monster in it is the open question.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>
  <EncounterCard
    id="Encounter 02 · Himalaya & Tibetan Plateau · 2017"
    title="The DNA Verdict"

    <p>
      In 2017, a team led by biologist Charlotte Lindqvist of the University at Buffalo carried out
      the most rigorous genetic analysis of alleged Yeti material to date. They examined nine
      specimens drawn from museums and private collections — bone, tooth, skin, hair, and fecal
      samples — including a tooth from a stuffed "Yeti" and a piece of skin from a supposed Yeti hand
      kept as a monastery relic. Several had been passed down for years as proof of the creature.
    </p>
    <p>
      The result was the one the pattern predicts. Eight of the nine samples were Asian bears — six
      Tibetan brown bears, one Himalayan brown bear, and one Asian black bear — and the ninth was a
      dog. Published in <em>Proceedings of the Royal Society B</em>, the study also produced the first
      genetic maps of the region's rare brown and black bears. "Our findings strongly suggest that the
      biological underpinnings of the Yeti legend can be found in local bears," Lindqvist concluded —
      the specimens meant to prove the creature became the clearest evidence against it.
    </p>
  </EncounterCard>

<Section heading="Before You Go">
  <Outro>
    <p>
      The Yeti is the best-documented monster in this guide and, in a way, the most instructive. It
      has a famous photograph, a century of expeditions, relics in monasteries, and a name known on
      every continent — and when each piece was finally measured, weighed, or sequenced, it resolved
      into a bear, a melting footprint, or a mistranslated headline.
    </p>
    <p>
      Respect the mountain, which is the thing up here that actually kills. Listen to the Sherpa, who
      named these beings with more care than the West ever did. And the next time you stand over an
      impossible footprint in warming snow, with the sun widening it by the minute —{' '}
      <em>photograph it, sample it, and believe the lab.</em>
    </p>
    <p>End of journal entry. Mind the altitude. Stay sceptical. Up here, the mountain is the monster.</p>
